## Chapter 1 — Six cases where claimed difference failed (pp. 9–38)

**Why the chapter exists.** Each case is a famous anthropological claim that some people lacked a trait
thought to be universal, or reversed it. Brown argues that each claim was wrong or overstated (pp. 9–11). He is
careful about what this shows. None of the corrections *proves* a universal, and he does not reject cultural
relativity as such. What the cases show is a professional readiness to accept claims of non-universality on
thin evidence (pp. 37–38).

### Case 1 · Color terms (pp. 11–14)
- **The claim.** Color vocabulary was the textbook case of arbitrary culture: a category grid imposed at random
  on a continuous physical spectrum (pp. 11–12).
- **The correction.** Berlin and Kay (1969) studied 98 languages. They found at most 11 basic color terms. Term
  boundaries vary, but the *focal points* are substantially the same across languages. Terms are added in a
  fixed order: black/white, then red, then green or yellow, and so on (pp. 12–13).
- **Biology.** Brown says the remaining task is to explain *why humans perceive colors uniformly*, and he cites
  work on the visual system (p. 14). He notes parallel evolutionary sequences in folk classification of plant
  and animal life-forms (p. 14).

### Case 2 · Samoan adolescence (pp. 14–20)
- **The claim.** Mead (1928) reported a stress-free Samoan adolescence, casual sex, little sexual jealousy,
  and rape as foreign (pp. 14–16). She attributed the absence of neurosis partly to households full of adults,
  so that children did not form close attachments to their own parents (p. 16).
- **The correction.** Freeman's later fieldwork found:
  - a cult of virginity and a sexual double standard;
  - surreptitious and forcible rape at high rates;
  - delinquency and severe corporal punishment (pp. 16–18).
- **Why Mead went wrong** (pp. 18–19):
  - she lacked the language;
  - she relied on adolescent girls as informants, and did not live with a Samoan family;
  - Samoans practiced recreational lying to outsiders;
  - she went hoping to show culture outweighed nature.
- **Brown's limit on the lesson** (pp. 19–20). The restudy does not prove that adolescent stress or sexual
  jealousy *is* universal. It removes Samoa as evidence that they are *not*. Other evidence suggests both are
  universal.

### Case 3 · Tchambuli/Chambri sex roles (pp. 20–23)
- **The claim.** Mead (1935) reported a reversal of sex temperaments (pp. 20–21).
- **The correction.** Gewertz's restudy (pp. 21–23) found that men had been seen as aggressive and women as
  submissive since at least the mid-1800s. Women were the breadwinners but never controlled the relations of
  production. Women were dominant only relative to *other* women.
- **Why Mead went wrong.** She saw a temporary condition. The people had recently returned from exile, and the
  men were rebuilding burned ritual structures, so normal male activities were muted (p. 22).
- **The general point.** Myths of a former matriarchy are widespread, but a diligent search found no actual
  matriarchy (p. 23).

### Case 4 · Facial expressions (pp. 23–27)
- **The claim.** La Barre and Birdwhistell denied any natural language of emotional gesture (pp. 23–24).
- **The correction.** Ekman's studies showed that facial expressions are recognized across cultures, including
  among the Fore and Dani, who had seen no films (pp. 24–26).
- **Ekman's "neuro-cultural" model** (pp. 24–25). An innate *facial affect program* is present in every nervous
  system. Learned *display rules* say when to show, mask, or exaggerate an expression. In one study Japanese
  and American subjects watching a stress film alone made the same faces. With another person present, the
  Japanese subjects masked their expressions with smiles.
- **Unsettled.** Which emotions count as basic is still disputed. Contempt was a late addition (p. 26).
- **Fridlund.** The primary function of human, *and animal*, facial expression may be to communicate
  **intention** in a social setting, rather than to express emotion. Brown treats the two views as compatible
  (p. 26).

### Case 5 · Hopi time (pp. 27–31)
- **The claim.** Whorf said the Hopi language had no notion of time.
- **The correction.** Malotki's 600-plus pages document Hopi time units, calendars, and tense. Hopi
  distinguishes future from nonfuture, where English distinguishes past from present (pp. 28–30).
- **Why Whorf went wrong.** His corpus was incomplete, and he had a mystical motive (pp. 29–30).
- **Brown's limit on the lesson** (p. 31). Extreme relativism must be rejected, not relativism.

### Case 6 · The Oedipus complex (pp. 32–37)
- **The claim.** Malinowski held that in the matrilineal Trobriands a boy's hostility goes to the mother's
  brother, not the father (pp. 32–33).
- **Spiro's reanalysis** (pp. 33–36). The Trobriand rearing conditions should intensify a classic triangle:
  - a boy has near-exclusive possession of his mother for about two years, sleeps with her, and nurses on
    demand;
  - a long postpartum sex taboo is in force;
  - the father resumes relations, in a one-room house, around the time of weaning.
- **Rearing can change and the complex persists.** In the kibbutz, children were reared collectively by
  specialists, with minimal parental involvement and easy sexual outlets among peers. Even so, fixation on the
  mother was not extinguished (p. 35).
- **Brown's conclusion** (p. 37). The triangular structure appears universal because its *conditions* (a boy,
  his mother, and her consort) are, as a societal norm, always present. Its strength and outcome vary:
  extinguished, repressed, or imperfectly repressed.

### Brown's general lesson (pp. 37–38)
The cases do not show that cultural variation is unreal. They show how claims of difference were accepted
because they fit the field's expectations.

## Chapter 3 — The historical context of the study of universals (pp. 54–87)

The chapter is a history of why universals were sidelined in American anthropology. Only the parts that bear on
biology-versus-culture explanation, or on animals, are extracted here.

### How biology was ruled out (pp. 55–66)
- **Boas and Kroeber.** Boas's culture concept was anti-racist. It explained differences by cultural history,
  not innate capacity (p. 55). Kroeber's "superorganic" made culture its own level. He called the relation of
  biological to social factors a "no-man's land" to be explored later (pp. 56–57). He later conceded only "a
  degree of autonomy," warning that "we have not yet proved that X equals 0" (p. 58).
- **Practice hardened into principle.** Brown argues that a long period of stressing cultural determinants *in
  practice* made many anthropologists think biology was excluded *in principle* (p. 58).
- **Wissler's "Universal Pattern" (1923).** It covered both classification and content, and rooted universals in
  a common human biology (pp. 58–59). Boas too allowed that universals not carried by early humans everywhere
  might be due to human nature (p. 59).
- **Durkheim.** He argued that alleged innate tendencies ("religious sentiment… sexual jealousy, filial piety,
  paternal love") are "often totally lacking," so psychology cannot explain social facts (p. 60).
- **Behaviorism.** It allowed only love, fear, and rage as instincts (Watson) (p. 60). Lashley's experiments on
  animals were read as showing that brain function is not localized, and research on brain localization stalled
  for decades (p. 61).
- **Evolutionary biology was unavailable** (p. 62). Darwinism was tainted by social Darwinism and eugenics, and
  the Fisher synthesis (1930) came too late to guide the field.
- **Murdock (1932): animals and humans.** He held that "all analogies" between animal and human societies are
  "never more than superficial." Yet he also said:
  - both animals and humans have society, and they differ only "by degree" of intelligence;
  - animals form habits;
  - apes have "fads," which fail to count as culture only because they last so briefly (p. 63).
  Murdock recanted his autonomy-of-culture position in 1972 (p. 63 n. 6).
- **Brown's correction: interaction.** Architecture, he notes, is always an interaction of cultural pattern
  with gravity (p. 63). If culture were truly autonomous, universals would be improbable, unless they were
  "cradle" traits inherited from humanity's infancy (pp. 63–64).
- **Kroeber's trap** (p. 64). Kroeber declared that if a trait is biologically determined, it is by definition
  not a *cultural* universal and so irrelevant to anthropology. He never said how to tell cultural traits from
  non-cultural ones, except by universality itself.
- **Douglas's taboo logic** (p. 64). A universal is "neither fish nor fowl": it straddles the nature/culture
  boundary, so it was hard to think about. Lévi-Strauss used this reasoning about the incest taboo (n. 7).
- **Benedict.** She judged some customs as running "counter to biological drives" while denying a fixed human
  nature (p. 65). She held that temperament types are universal and that cultures label them normal or
  abnormal (pp. 65–66).
- **Goldschmidt: norms vary more than behavior** (p. 66 n. 9). Anthropologists put weight on norms rather than
  actual behavior. Actual behavior, "a product of a human nature that is not entirely constrained by culture,"
  varies less than norms do.

### Universalist frameworks resting on biological needs (pp. 66–71)
- **Malinowski.** His seven "principles of integration" are:
  reproduction, territoriality, physiology, voluntary association, occupation, rank, and the comprehensive
  (pp. 66–67). He held that "any theory of culture has to start with the organic needs of man" (p. 67), and
  mapped basic needs to cultural responses (p. 67):

  | Basic need | Cultural response |
  |---|---|
  | metabolism | commissariat |
  | reproduction | kinship |
  | bodily comforts | shelter |
  | safety | protection |
  | movement | activities |
  | growth | training |
  | health | hygiene |

  Brown calls this a list of universal *conditions* for society, not a list of universals (p. 67).
- **Murdock (1945).** His partial list of universals is on pp. 69–70. He placed their basis in "the fundamental
  biological and psychological nature of man and in the universal conditions of human existence." His HRAF
  categories are "a grand sort of universal pool" (p. 70).
- **Bidney** (pp. 70–71). He rejected culture as autonomous: "Omnis cultura ex natura."
- **Herskovits** (p. 71). His alternative to biological needs was "historic phenomena": traits so ancient they
  spread *with* humanity.

### The return of biology (pp. 72–87)
- **White (1948)** revived Tylor's argument about the incest taboo: groups that failed to marry out died out
  (p. 72).
- **Kluckhohn (1953).** Primate and human neuroanatomy suggested that some behaviors depend "less on
  sociocultural factors than had previously been thought" (p. 72). He insisted that biologically grounded
  universals are still socially transmitted, and are "likenesses, not identities" (p. 73).
- **Hallowell (1963).** Anthropology paid lip service to psychic unity. It also emphasized human uniqueness and
  so dodged evolution. He called for a comparative psychology linking human and *animal* studies (pp. 73–74).
- **Geertz (1965).** Bodies, brains, and cultures coevolved, so humans are "incomplete" without culture. In this
  view humans traded detailed genetic control for more general control (pp. 74–75). Brown's reply (p. 75):
  - dependence on *some* cultural items (tools) is not dependence on culture in general;
  - Symons argues that the very *rarity* of polyandry, and the conditions that produce it, are evidence for
    innate sex differences.
- **Linguistics** (pp. 77–80):
  - The arbitrariness of the sign has been exaggerated (n. 12).
  - Chomsky called speech an organ acquired "as naturally as pubic hair." He likened it to instinctive
    behaviors in "lower animals" acquired by "imprinting" during sharply limited periods (pp. 77–78).
  - Greenberg's marking universals: the unmarked term is "good," "long," "deep," "many" (pp. 78–79).
  - In kinship terms, every language has separate terms for father and mother, and none obligatorily merges
    them. All kin terminologies use at least generation and sex (pp. 79–80).
- **Tiger and Fox (1971)** (p. 81):
  - a human "biogrammar," built from evolution, ethology, the fossil record, and anthropology;
  - Tiger's male bonding as a hunting adaptation;
  - the **"experimental Adam and Eve"** thought experiment: a pair raised apart from culture would, within a few
    generations, have descendants who replicate the universal pattern.
  - Fox's claim that the important universals are at the level of *process*: "the universal process of
    reproduction… may or may not give rise to 'families'."
- **Mead (1972)** conceded, from new work on primates, ungulates, and birds, a willingness to "lay more
  emphasis on man's specific biological inheritance" (p. 81 n. 15).
- **Sociobiology** (pp. 82–83):
  - Hamilton, Maynard Smith, Trivers, and Wilson's work;
  - the conviction that selection acts on individuals or genes, not on groups or species;
  - kin selection "rang bells": it explained the universality of kinship and nepotism, and reciprocal
    altruism explained reciprocity.
- **Ethology** (p. 83) supplies observations of behaviors with human analogues in "numerous animal species,"
  plus methods. It has documented elusive universals of *content*:
  - Eibl-Eibesfeldt's coyness display;
  - normal smiling, laughing, and crying among thalidomide children born without sight, hearing, or normal
    limbs.
- **Selection acts on psychology, not behavior** (p. 83 n. 16). Selection acts on the psychological processes
  that underlie behavior. In a novel environment those processes can produce behavior that makes no sense in
  terms of reproductive success. Sociobiological leaps from "maximize reproduction" to specific customs
  (e.g., female infanticide) are criticized.
- **The Garcia effect** (p. 84). Rats easily learn to associate taste with nausea, and light or sound with shock.
  They hardly learn the crossed pairings. So the brain is structured before conditioning, and Brown calls that
  structure an evolutionary product. The paper was rejected by top journals.
- **Learning versus acquisition** (pp. 84–85):
  - Gould and Marler's "learning by instinct," for bird song and human speech: adaptations that still need
    experience or imprinting to develop;
  - preparedness: one-trial learning of food aversions, and phobias as prepared learning (Seligman).
- **A specialized brain** (pp. 85–86):
  - visual-cortex specialization, down to neurons responding to faces seen from a given angle;
  - AI shows that even simple tasks need task-specific "innate knowledge."
  - Evolution posed specific problems, not general ones, so there is no general-purpose mental organ.
  - Specialization yields fixed responses (coyness, the smile), aims ("look after close kin"), and inclinations
    ("be wary around snakes").
- **Summary** (pp. 86–87). The pendulum is swinging back toward interaction. Brown also notes that the
  nature/culture dichotomy may have folk roots in the flesh/spirit dichotomy (n. 17).

## Chapter 5 — Incest avoidance (pp. 118–129)

The chapter's real subject is **brother-sister** incest, and a line of research testing whether siblings avoid
incest *by nature* (p. 119). Brown uses it as his model of how a universal should be explained (pp. 128–129).

### Misconceptions cleared first (pp. 118–119)
- **Sex, not marriage.** Marriage rules were conflated with sexual regulation. Brown: "incest fundamentally
  concerns sex, only coincidentally may it concern marriage" (p. 118).
- **Animals.** The taboo was assumed to mark humanity off from animals, which were thought to mate
  incestuously. Brown: "It is now known that incest is rare among animals in the wild (domestic animals, whose
  breeding patterns have been altered by human interference, are another matter)." This opens a continuity that
  was previously denied (p. 118).
- **Not literally universal.**
  - In some societies royalty were enjoined to commit incest, or at least to marry very close kin (p. 119).
  - Evidence that royal sibling *marriage* led to actual reproduction is very limited. Arens suggests the motive
    was to keep royal sisters off the marriage market, so that no rival heirs were born (p. 119 n. 2).
  - Some societies have no explicit rules, only a notion that no one would do it (p. 119).
- **Dyads lumped together.** Different dyads (brother-sister, father-daughter, mother-son) were wrongly run
  together (p. 119).

### Freud versus Westermarck (pp. 119–120)
- **Freud's view.** Incest is tabooed because people are drawn to it.
- **Westermarck's view.** Most people are *not* drawn to it. There is "a remarkable lack of erotic feeling
  between persons who have been living closely together from childhood" (Westermarck 1922:192). Such persons
  are usually relatives, so avoidance is a natural tendency arising from **childhood association**, not from
  knowledge of relatedness (p. 119).
- **Westermarck's answer to Freud's objection.** We taboo incest for the same reason we taboo bestiality and
  parricide: some individuals go awry in ways that shock general sentiment (p. 119).
- **His Darwinian basis.** Westermarck held that inbreeding is directly harmful, and that the association-based
  avoidance is an evolved instinct. Anthropology's opposition to biological reductionism eclipsed this view
  (p. 120).

### Natural experiments in co-rearing (pp. 120–123)

**Kibbutz peer groups** (p. 120):
- **Setup.** Children close in age were raised together in peer groups (*kvutza*) of 6–8, in shared quarters
  from shortly after birth through adolescence. Nurses and teachers raised them, not parents.
- **Result.** As small children they showed typical sexual interest in each other, which disappeared as they
  matured. Spiro (1958) found not one marriage, nor even sexual intercourse, between children raised together
  from childhood in the same peer group.

**Fox's synthesis** (Fox 1962; pp. 120–121):
- **Inverse rule.** Close physical intimacy among co-socialized children produces sexual disinterest after
  puberty: "the intensity of heterosexual attraction between cosocialized children after puberty is inversely
  proportionate to the intensity of heterosexual activity between them before puberty."
- **Rearing practice predicts taboo strength.**
  - Kibbutz-like societies are relatively indifferent to incest. They disapprove of it but need not punish it
    stringently. Examples are the Tallensi, Pondo, Mountain Arapesh, Tikopia, and a Chinese case.
  - Societies whose rearing keeps siblings apart need stringent taboos to overcome real desire. Examples are
    the Chiricahua Apache and the Trobriands.
- **Other studies.** Talmon (1964) confirmed the effect in three more communes (p. 121).

**Chinese "minor marriage"** (Wolf; Wolf and Huang 1980; pp. 121–122):
- **Setup.** A young girl was adopted into the family of her future husband, partly to ease mother-in-law
  strain. The couple were unrelated but were raised like siblings.
- **Outcomes, compared with "major" marriages:**
  - about 30% less fertile and less happy;
  - more prostitutes, mistresses, and affairs by husbands, and more affairs by wives;
  - more separation and divorce.
- **Spontaneous avoidance.** When economic change weakened parental control, couples avoided such marriages on
  their own.
- **Alternative readings tested.** Major-marriage brides were *more* mistreated. Uxorilocal marriages, which
  carry lower prestige, were more fertile and stable than minor ones (p. 122).
- **Unaware of the cost.** Informants did not know minor marriages were less fertile (p. 122).
- **Chicago sibling incest** (Weinberg 1963). The only offenders who had contemplated marriage were siblings
  raised apart (p. 121).

**Lebanese parallel-cousin marriage** (McCabe 1983; p. 122):
- **Setup.** Father's-brother's-daughter cousins grew up "in an association as close as that of siblings,"
  including childhood sexual exploration.
- **Outcomes.** Their marriages had 23% fewer children in the first 25 years and were four times as likely to
  end in divorce. It is parents, not the couples, who prefer such matches.

**The age window** (pp. 122–123):
- **Wolf and Huang.** Minor marriages in which the pair were brought together before age 4 were twice as likely
  to end in divorce as those where the pair met at 8 or later (p. 122).
- **Shepher (1983).** He studied 2,769 couples from 211 kibbutzim (pp. 122–123):
  - only 14 marriages were alleged to be within a peer group, and all of them dissolved on checking;
  - **no** marriage occurred between a boy and girl who had spent their first six years in the same peer group;
  - there was no premarital sex among those raised together from infancy;
  - children who *joined* the group later sometimes felt intense attraction.
- **No prohibition was involved.** Children's sex play was not stopped, and dating or marrying commune mates was
  not forbidden; intracommunal marriage was even encouraged (p. 123).
- **Shepher's conclusion** (p. 123). The effect is **negative imprinting**: it is complete by age 6, takes about 4
  years, and is a phylogenetic adaptation to reduce the harm of inbreeding.

### The cost of inbreeding, and avoidance in other species (pp. 123–124)
- **In humans.** Shepher's review of the human evidence (p. 123):
  - full-sibling or parent-child matings give about 17% child mortality and 25% disability, about 42% nonviable
    offspring in all;
  - the costs fall off rapidly for more distant kin.
- **The population-level objection** (p. 123 n. 3). Some argue that populations need not suffer, or that
  sustained inbreeding purges harmful genes. Both arguments ignore "the immediate disadvantages for those most
  immediately involved" (Arens).
- **In other mammals** (p. 124). Ralls, Ballou, and Templeton (1988) studied **38 captive mammalian species**. Close
  incestuous matings gave an average offspring mortality of about 33%, measured as juvenile survival. The range
  ran from 0 to nearly 100% across species.
- **Three avoidance mechanisms** (Shepher; p. 124):
  1. **Prohibition**: rules. Only humans have it.
  2. **Prevention**: mating simply cannot happen. For example, parents die before their offspring are fertile,
     or siblings disperse widely.
  3. **Inhibition**: close, fertile kin are near each other but do not mate, via imprinting or similar processes.
- **Animal evidence** (p. 124). Prevention and inhibition are widely reported across animal species (Bischof
  1972). Parent-offspring and sibling incest in the wild is "apparently rather rare" (Lewin 1989).
- **The "double discontinuity."** Holding that human avoidance is purely cultural now requires "the inelegant
  assumption of a double discontinuity with the animal kingdom": that humans alone lack the innate mechanism,
  and humans alone use prohibition instead (p. 124, after Arens).

### Other dyads (pp. 124–125)
- **Mother-son.** Shepher argues, on minimal evidence, that it is also inhibited by imprinting, given the long,
  intimate mother-child contact (p. 124).
- **Father-daughter.** In many societies this dyad has the fewest opportunities to develop aversion (p. 124).
- **Frequencies.** Father-daughter incest is commoner than mother-son, and stepfather-stepdaughter incest is
  disproportionately common. That is the order imprinting predicts (p. 125).
- **Parker and Parker (1986)** (p. 125):
  - abusive fathers had been "much less frequently involved in caring and nurturing activities" in the
    daughter's first three years;
  - step- and adoptive fathers were more often abusive;
  - biological fathers with early contact similar to step-fathers' showed no significant difference.
  This extends Westermarck beyond siblings: early **care contact**, not genealogy, predicts inhibition.
- **The family-disruption hypothesis fails** (pp. 124–125). It held that incest wrecks the family. Studies of
  real cases undercut it. Bagley found "functional incest," which *maintained* family integrity, in 93 of 425
  cases.

### The counterexample: Roman Egypt (pp. 125–127)
- **Royal precedent.** A Ptolemaic king married his full sister, and 7 of the next 11 Greek kings married sisters
  (p. 126).
- **Commoners** (p. 126). Roman census returns (AD 19/20–257/258) give 172 usable returns covering 880 persons:
  - 17 of 113 ongoing marriages were certainly brother-sister, and 6 more possibly were (about 15–21%);
  - 11–12 were full siblings and 8 half-siblings;
  - only about 40% of families would have had a marriageable brother-sister pair, so a third or more of those
    who *could* marry a sibling did.
- **Treated as normal.** Wedding invitations, letters, and contracts treat these marriages as normal, with signs
  of affection. "Sister" was also a term of endearment, which weakens some of the evidence (p. 126).
- **No sign of known harm** (p. 126). The marriages were fertile, and no source shows awareness of genetic harm.
  Brown asks whether high infant mortality masked the inbreeding cost, as the Chinese were also unaware of
  theirs.
- **Hopkins's explanation is cultural** (p. 127):
  - the Isis-Osiris sibling-spouse myth;
  - a romantic tradition of sibling love;
  - women's high status and autonomy in choosing and leaving marriages;
  - not dowry avoidance.
  The practice ended when Egyptians became Roman citizens in AD 212–213.
- **Probably not co-reared** (p. 127). The five couples with known ages were separated by 7, 8, 4, 8, and 20
  years. So with one exception they were probably not co-reared in the way that triggers the effect.
- **Shepher's reply** (p. 127). He dismissed the case: one exception carries little weight, and since nature
  works by probability, a near-universal is what to expect. Brown notes that this reverses anthropology's usual
  view that one exception defeats a universal.

### Criticisms (pp. 127–128)
- **Spiro: adolescence, not infancy.** It is adolescent sexual repression, not childhood rearing, that drives
  kibbutz peers apart. Kaffman reports peer liaisons once adolescent controls relaxed, but gives no data
  (pp. 127–128).
- **Shepher's rebuttal.** Marriages between people who were peers only in adolescence did occur before (p. 128).
- **Brown's own criticism** (p. 128). Shepher's 4-year window in the first 6 years can only be a *small part* of
  the controls. Commune members who were *not* in the same peer group also seldom marry each other, so other
  factors are at work.
- **Spiro's further points** (p. 128). Peer groups are small, so marrying out is statistically expected. Peer
  groups are also same-age, and girls tend to prefer older boys.

### Brown's conclusions (pp. 128–129)
- **Taboo and avoidance are different claims.** "The incest taboo clearly is not universal, though it surely is
  a statistical universal and might be a near-universal. On the other hand, incest avoidance may be universal"
  (p. 128).
- **Ethology reframed the problem.** "The ethological discovery that humans are far from unique in avoiding
  incest has entirely reoriented the problem" (p. 128).
- **A partial explanation only.** The Westermarck hypothesis is a successfully tested explanation for *part* of
  the phenomenon. It may not survive, and it does not preclude complementary explanations (pp. 128–129).
- **The model method.** The method is the lesson: identify the ultimate (evolutionary) conditions, specify the
  proximate mechanism (infant negative imprinting producing a psychological state), and exploit natural
  experiments and quantitative tests. This approach, involving "ethological and evolutionary perspectives, a
  detailed specification of mechanisms and of individual motivation, a diligent search for natural experiments,
  and quantitative tests when possible—deserves emulation with other universals" (p. 129).

## Biology, animals, and evolution across the book (not in the existing extraction)

### Preface and Introduction
- **Brown lost a bet** (pp. vii–viii). He bet Donald Symons he could find a society reversing each of Symons's
  pan-human sex differences in sexuality, such as quicker and more visually cued male arousal. He could not.
  He had started from "a simplistic formulation in which human invariants may plausibly be explained in
  biological terms, while the variables demand cultural explanations," and he no longer accepts it (p. viii).
- **Five theses** (pp. 5–6):
  1. universals exist and are important;
  2. they are heterogeneous;
  3. they were tabooed by doctrines from 1915–1934;
  4. many universals reflect human biology fairly directly;
  5. evolutionary psychology is key, with the human mind as the most interesting part of biology.

### Chapter 2: what the list leaves out, and why
- **Obvious biology is excluded by design** (pp. 39, 41). Anthropological lists rarely include features thought
  straightforwardly anatomical or physiological. A universal of anthropological interest must not be too
  obviously or completely determined by biology. "All humans breathe, and yet no list of universals mentions
  the fact." Sexual activity is likewise left out, though incest rules and sex differences are included
  (p. 41). The line between "thoroughly genetically determined" and "not too obviously biological" is simply
  the line of what anthropologists have found interesting (p. 42).
- **Culture, defined** (p. 40). Culture is conventional patterns passed on by learning, not by "specific genetic
  programming," both vertically and horizontally. It can take distinct forms among genetically
  indistinguishable peoples.
- **Sign languages** (p. 41 n. 1). Deaf sign languages lack phonemes but have analogous units below the
  morpheme.
- **Overridden tendencies** (pp. 43–44). Men nearly universally find lighter skin pigmentation attractive in
  women (van den Berghe, with an evolutionary explanation). Where tanning signals status, that preference is
  overridden. Many tendencies exist, and few manifest as absolutes.
- **The dog** (pp. 44, 50). The domestic dog was absent from "probably less than 5 percent" of known cultures,
  so a 95% cutoff "would" make it a near-universal (p. 44). Brown lists "the dog and metal tools" as candidates,
  just as likely as tobacco, for *new* universals (p. 50).
- **Statistical universals with a perceptual basis** (pp. 44–45). About a third of languages derive "pupil" from
  "little person," from the reflection seen in another's eye. About 20% derive "muscle" from a small animal,
  as English does from Latin *mus*, "mouse," because the two move alike.
- **Counting** (p. 46). Following Hale, the *capacity* to count is an innate universal even where number systems
  are minimal.
- **Life-form sequences** (p. 46). Folk classification of plant and animal life-forms develops in universal
  evolutionary sequences.
- **Process over manifest form** (p. 47). Following Fox, the nuclear family is not universal, but "the bonding
  processes on which it is based" are. Fox's four kinship principles: women have the children; men impregnate
  the women; men usually exercise control; primary kin do not mate (p. 47).
- **Universals of essence** (p. 49). "The purest examples… are those strictly biological features of the species
  that do not generally receive much anthropological attention." The examples are Brown's, where HUX gives only
  the definition. Essence universals at the individual level collectively constitute human nature. Essence
  versus accident is a continuum, and the debate over sex differences is a debate over which side they fall on
  (p. 50).
- **Former universals** (p. 50):
  - Brown's list includes "relatively rare experiences of menstruation" (HUX omits it).
  - "Since many former universals were part of the environment in which human nature evolved," they matter for
    understanding that nature.

### Chapter 4: biology HUX does not carry
- **Handedness** (p. 91). It is partly genetic, with environmental influences that appear to be prenatal.
  Forcing right-handed writing does not change the hand used for throwing or striking. No one argues for an
  innate *positive feeling* about the right.
- **Biological clocks** (p. 93). "Since humans, along with myriad other species, have built-in biological clocks
  of various sorts, it is not at all clear that the conception of time is ever fundamentally cultural."
- **The full givens list** (p. 94). Brown's mode-3 givens are: "large-brained, slow-maturing, sexually
  reproducing, group-living, terrestrial, omnivorous, and often quarrelsome **mammals** with moderate sexual
  dimorphism and no estrus." HUX drops "mammals" and "often." Brown also says this mode is thought
  "self-evident." The causal chain "is not traced in detail" and is not tested, so it is "inherently weak or
  limited" (pp. 94, 117).
- **Fire and cooking** (p. 95):
  - fire-making is 40,000–100,000 years old (HUX says ~40,000), and fire *use* is about 1.5 million years old;
  - fire's uses include illumination and purification;
  - fire and cooking "lack direct counterparts outside the hominid line" and show no spontaneous emergence;
  - yet their utility rests on human physiology: body temperature, vulnerability to predators, the digestive
    system, and microbes;
  - humans may have adapted physiologically to cooking, for example in dentition and digestive enzymes.
- **Economy of effort** (p. 98). "Economy and efficiency are universal characteristics of biological mechanisms"
  (Williams).
- **Curiosity** is lifelong in humans (p. 99, Eibl-Eibesfeldt).
- **Fixed action patterns versus drives** (p. 101):
  - Ethologists' "fixed action patterns" are released by "releasers."
  - Human social behavior has nothing like "the neuroanatomical fixity of animal behaviors." The few exceptions
    are the infant's sucking reflex, certain facial expressions, and the coyness display.
  - So what selection fixes in humans are "ends, goals, motives, or drives. These ends or drives might be fixed
    but not the actions they give rise to."
- **Population differences** (p. 101 n. 5). Evolution has not stopped, and some Paleolithic features may have
  deteriorated. But adaptation to specific environments "can only account for racial or population
  differences, not new features of human nature."
- **Facultative adaptation** (p. 103 n. 6). Because humans have phylogenetic facultative adaptations, the notion
  "that if something varies it must be cultural" is obsolete: "If it varies in a regular pattern, it may well
  not be cultural." HUX has obligate/facultative but not this consequence.
- **Level of selection** (p. 103). Traits "should be explained in terms of the way they make individuals fit,
  not in terms of group or species benefits." Social scientists often misattribute adaptation to groups or
  species. HUX omits this.
- **Phylogenetic constraint** (p. 104). Constraints "make it much more likely that smell might become our
  paramount sense of perception than that we fletch [*sic*] and fly." The point is about which evolutionary
  directions are *open*. HUX (HUX:434-439) renders it as humans relying on smell less than ideal design would
  predict, which is not Brown's example.
- **Short-term gain** (p. 104). Selection can yield adaptations with short-term advantage and long-term cost.
- **Complementary filiation** (p. 105, after Fortes). Even in strictly patrilineal or matrilineal systems,
  people keep strong sentimental ties to close genetic kin that the ideology does not count. So kin sentiment is
  effectively bilateral, which Brown calls a clue to the biological basis of nepotism. Adoption and
  domestic-homicide data also document nepotism (p. 105).
- **Social insects** (pp. 105–106). Kin selection was worked out for sterile castes of bees, ants, and wasps,
  which serve very close kin.
- **The fitness calculator** (pp. 106–107). The criticism applies to "the human mind—or any other mind, for that
  matter." Humans "(and other species)" faced specific problems, not general ones. Example: men's evolved
  mechanism is arousal at cues of nubility, not a drive to reproduce, which is why, given contraception, men
  forgo reproduction more readily than sex (Symons).
- **Distant kin** (p. 107). It is unclear that humans "(or any other species)" can distinguish degrees of
  distant kin. Explaining ethnic or racial sentiment as kin selection is "rightly criticized."
- **Brown's three narrow kin mechanisms** (p. 107) are: (1) incest avoidance; (2) male sexual jealousy, as a
  mechanism to avoid investing in another man's offspring; (3) the mother-infant bond, "probably a complex of
  adaptations both in mothers and in infants." HUX (HUX:416-417) lists "parent-infant bond, incest avoidance,
  sibling defense."
- **Stranger recognition** (p. 107 n. 7). The mechanisms are probably triggered by personal unfamiliarity, and
  also by cultural markers (language, accent, body adornment), which feeds ethnocentrism. In ancestral
  environments, familiar versus stranger correlated with degree of kinship.
- **Parental investment detail** (pp. 108–109):
  - the higher-investing sex is choosier, and the lower-investing sex is larger, more colorful, and more
    competitive;
  - the size of the investment *difference* scales with the size of the sex difference;
  - Brown traces it to the cost of the ovum versus the sperm, enhanced in mammals by internal gestation and
    lactation.
- **Seahorses and pipefish: the reversal** (p. 109 n. 9). In these species the females insert their eggs into
  males, which fertilize and brood them. **Females are promiscuous and aggressive, and males are choosy.** So the
  sex-difference pattern follows *who invests*, not which sex is which.
- **The Aka** (p. 109). Even among the Aka, whose fathers invest unusually heavily, mothers provide considerably
  more direct childcare.
- **Hormones and stature** (p. 110). Hormonal sex differences appear "from earliest infancy." Male aggressiveness
  is the behavioral counterpart of greater stature. Male public dominance may follow from the size difference,
  from the propensity to violence, and from gestation and lactation being greater handicaps to political action
  than insemination is.
- **Dispositions and susceptibilities** (p. 111, Sperber). A disposition can become a susceptibility in a new
  environment, as the disposition to eat sweets becomes a susceptibility to overconsume sugar.
- **Why groups** (pp. 111–112). Group living costs something, such as disease transmission. Alexander's three
  factors are: predator protection, efficient resource use, and highly localized resources. Of these, it is
  "the first factor that primarily accounts for group living in primates, including humans."
- **Kin recognition requires individual recognition** (p. 112). "Kinship requires the ability to recognize kin,
  which entails the ability to recognize individuals, an ability possessed by many species. Recognition by face
  is the commonest means employed by humans."
- **Snake wariness** (p. 115). It is shared with other primates. The evidence: animal counterparts, extreme ease
  of acquisition, difficulty of suppression, an emotional rather than rational basis, and its sense in natural
  environments. Peoples who revere snakes are still wary of them.
- **Sensory partial explanations** (pp. 114–116):
  - percussion at ritual transitions (Needham);
  - the vestibular "assault on the equilibrium sense" in trance, and in swings, merry-go-rounds, and whirling
    games (Gell);
  - **infrasonic** waves from bull-roarers and large drums producing an "uncanny" feeling (Tuzin);
  - endorphins.
- **Habitat preference** (p. 116, Orians). Humans prefer the settings that would have been optimal for
  Pleistocene foragers, and the argument draws on habitat selection in other species, "where its innate
  component is less questionable."
- **Poetic lines** (p. 116; also p. 150). The ~3-second poetic line is explained by brain information-processing
  features (Turner and Pöppel). The checklist's U-009 says the extraction gives no cause; Brown gives one.
- **Mode 9 is formal, not causal** (pp. 116–117). Brown classes interspecific comparison with modes 1, 10, and 11
  as essentially formal or methodological: compatible with the other modes, not a cause in itself.

### Chapter 6
- **Culture is not uniquely human** (p. 130). "Although humans are not unique in their possession of
  culture…they certainly are unique in the extent to which their thought and action are shaped by such
  patterns."
- **Alarm calls versus words** (p. 131). Many bird species have danger calls that are much the same across
  locations and even across species. Humans have cries of fright analogous to these. But most human
  vocalizations have only an arbitrary link between sound and sense.
- **Toolmaking is not uniquely human** (p. 135). "Some other animals do too." What is distinctive is the variety
  of tools, the dependence on them, and using tools to make tools.
- **Emic testimony set aside** (p. 140). Brown counted empathy as universal despite reports, from the Zapotec and
  the Kaluli, of peoples who deny that others' minds can be known. He judged such reports emically correct but
  not etically.

### Chapter 7
- **Comparison across species** (p. 145). Anthropology compares "even across species." Humans are an evolved
  species "as with other organisms." The mind is the same in all populations "barring sex and age differences."
- **Instinct blends with learning** (p. 147). Proposition 2 ignores "the vast array of animal behaviors,
  including some human behaviors, in which instincts blend with learning to result in entirely natural
  behaviors (speaking and smiling, for example, among humans)." Humans have few fixed action patterns. That
  does not make the rest learned without genes selected to facilitate the learning.
- **Twin studies miss species-typical traits** (p. 147 n. 2). Behavior genetics (twin studies) cannot detect
  genes that do not vary, and stable species-typical adaptations are exactly those.
- **Conditional responses** (p. 148). Proposition 3 wrongly assumes there are no mechanisms specifying different
  responses to different inputs.
- **Playfighting** (p. 148 n. 3). Children everywhere playfight. Among some peoples they don't actually strike;
  among others they do. "Any innate bases of playfighting should figure in the explanation." Peaceableness may
  itself be a conditional adaptation: "act tough when you can get away with it, but when you can't get away with
  it, act mild." The manifest difference is still an indirect consequence of human nature.
- **Culture compared to animals** (p. 148). "When compared with other animal species it is correct to say that
  culture is particularly important in shaping human affairs." But no method exists to quantify how much.
- **Comparative study** (p. 150). Comparative study illuminates unrestricted universals, "just what has happened
  with the incest taboo, now seen as incest avoidance." Conjectural reasoning "evolved with hunting" (after
  Ginzburg). Brain-damage deficits might map onto specific universals, such as losing the grasp of reciprocity.
- **Pseudospeciation** (pp. 152–153). Hereditary stratification is everywhere justified by the notion "that
  humanity is not a single species, because the rulers (at least) are superhuman, quasidivine, or divine
  entities with inherently different character from lower humans." Caste-organized societies deny psychic
  unity, "a kind of racism or pseudospeciation." Societies with open stratification affirm it. Brown also found
  "an apparently universal concern with rank or inequality."
- **Innate endowment and totalitarianism** (p. 155 n. 5). The nightmare is that people are "so programmable that
  they could be reduced to social automatons." Chomsky counters that an innate endowment is a defense against
  totalitarianism.

## Implications for non-human races

### 1. Incest avoidance (U-069) must be re-derived as three mechanisms, not one item
**Brown basis:** pp. 118–129. **M-3:** Steps 3–5, 8. **L1:** reproduction, maturation, social instinct,
cognition (P-2, P-5, the proposed cognitive-profile parameter).

- **Split the item.** Brown separates two claims (p. 128):
  - **avoidance**: etic, perhaps universal, with a biological mechanism;
  - **taboo**: emic, a statistical universal only.

  M-3 should treat U-069 as at least two rows:
  - **U-069a · Avoidance by inhibition**: a formal/process candidate for L2, *if* L1 keeps an association-based
    inhibition mechanism.
  - **U-069b · Prohibition (the taboo)**: substantive, free to vary, L3/L4.

  Prevention (dispersal, mortality) belongs in the L1 life history. It may make inhibition less necessary.
- **The trigger is association, not genealogy.** The natural experiments all say the same thing: co-rearing in
  intimate early contact produces inhibition between *unrelated* children (kibbutz, minor marriage), and
  genealogical kin reared apart don't get it (Chicago). This has three consequences.
  - **Litters.** If L1 keeps a Westermarck-type mechanism, litter-mates are its strongest possible case:
    same-age, intimate, from birth. The *human* data are about children of different ages. Whether same-age
    co-rearing behaves the same is not established by Brown, and Spiro raised age-preference as a confound
    (p. 128).
  - **Communal or facility rearing (L0, first generation).** If a lineage's founders were raised in cohorts by
    their creators, as in kibbutz peer groups, Brown's evidence predicts **cohort-wide mutual sexual disinterest
    among the founders.** That would push mating *out of* the rearing group. With a small founding population
    and several rearing cohorts this shapes founder genetics and early L3 social structure. **This is a key L0
    question to ask the developer**: how were the founders reared, and in what groups? (Log as a `Q-`.)
  - **The sensitive window is a human developmental figure.** Shepher has completion by age 6, over about 4
    years; Wolf finds the effect strongest before age 4. M-3 must not copy the ages. It should re-derive the
    window from P-5 (maturation) as "the early-childhood intimate-contact period," and log it UNCERTAIN until L1
    defines the developmental stages.
- **Cue channel.** Brown's framing is channel-neutral: association, intimacy, care. He notes that human
  individual recognition is mostly by face (p. 112). For a race whose L1 gives scent primacy, familiarity may be
  olfactory (verify per base under M-2). Treat this as an **ANALOG** of the human mechanism, or as
  **ANIMAL-INHERITED** if the base animal has its own inhibition mechanism that L1 keeps (verify).
- **Dyad asymmetry follows care contact** (pp. 124–125). Brown's evidence ranks the inhibition: strongest
  mother-son, weaker father-daughter, weakest stepfather. M-3 should derive the race's ranking from **who
  provides early care**: P-2 paternal care, alloparenting, crèche rearing. The human order should not be copied.
  A race with little paternal care and no paternal recognition predicts weak father-daughter inhibition. It
  would then rely on prevention (dispersal) or prohibition.
- **The cost of inbreeding is species-specific** (p. 124). Across 38 mammal species, mortality from close
  incestuous matings ran from 0 to nearly 100%.
  - **For L1:** inbreeding depression is a parameter to source per base (M-2) and per engineered genome. It is
    not a constant. Engineered founders may have designed-in genetic diversity, or the reverse (M-4).
  - **Small founder populations** make this acute. It ties to HAR-HUF-24 (population-level divergence) and
    M-7.
- **Domestic animals are a poor guide** (p. 118). Brown flags domestic breeding as "altered by human
  interference." This reinforces EBEM's domestication caution. For dog and rabbit bases, wild or feral
  populations, not domestic breeds, are the right reference for avoidance mechanisms (verify).
- **Rearing practice predicts taboo strength** (Fox, pp. 120–121). This is an **implicational universal**: if
  co-rearing is intimate, the taboo is weak; if siblings are kept apart, the taboo is stringent. It is a
  ready-made **Differentiation Engine condition** for L3/L4: rearing mode leads to taboo stringency. It routes
  through a mechanism plus a condition, which satisfies `CLAUDE.md` rule 3.
- **People can be unaware of costs** (Chinese, Egyptian; pp. 122, 126). Don't assume a race "knows" about
  inbreeding harm, even with engineered origins. That knowledge is a creator-culture item (L3), not a biological
  given.
- **Robot verdict reversal.** The robot pass made U-069 a Non-Transfer. For a biological race all three
  mechanisms come back, and inhibition may be the *strongest* of them for litter species. Do not copy.

### 2. Kin recognition, nepotism, and the "narrow range" (U-068, U-185, HAR-HUF-29)

- **Brown's own three narrow mechanisms** (p. 107) are incest avoidance, male sexual jealousy, and the
  mother-infant bond. HAR-HUF-29 inherited HUX's version, which swaps in "parent-infant" and "sibling defense."
  HAR-HUF-29 should be corrected (this file proposes; it does not edit).
- **Mother-infant bond** (p. 107). Brown calls it "a complex of adaptations both in mothers and in infants."
  For litter species, M-3 should ask whether the infant-side adaptations are per-offspring or litter-level, and
  whether the mother-side bond is to individuals or to the nest (verify per base).
- **Individual recognition.** Kin recognition requires individual recognition, which "many species" have

- **Complementary filiation** (p. 105). Sentiment ignores the ideology of descent and stays effectively

### 3. Sex differences follow investment, not sex (U-076–U-081, U-145–U-146, U-150, HAR-HUF-22, HAR-HUF-30)
**Brown basis:** pp. 108–110, especially the seahorse/pipefish note (p. 109 n. 9). **M-3:** Steps 3–4. **L1:**
reproduction (P-2), the proposed sex-differences parameter.
- **The root cause is investment asymmetry.** Brown's own example shows the human pattern *reverses* when males
  invest more. So the root-cause check for every sex-difference item is "which sex invests more, and by how
  much?" It is not "is there a male and a female?"
- **Consequences:**
  - a race with designed or inherited high paternal investment, or with communal rearing that equalizes
    investment, can show reduced or reversed patterns (verify per base; M-4 for design);
  - this is a **Direct Transfer, re-parameterized** case (HAR-HUF-03): same mechanism, different numbers;
  - the Aka note (p. 109) warns that even high paternal investment may leave the gestating and lactating sex
    ahead in direct care.
- **Public dominance** (U-081, U-150). Brown roots it partly in size dimorphism and in gestation and lactation
  as handicaps (p. 110). A race with low dimorphism, short gestation, or shared lactation weakens those causes.
  It does not automatically reverse them.
- **Number of sexes.** Brown's sex terminology is "fundamentally dualistic" even where 3–4 categories exist
  (pp. 133–134; new **U-179**). For aliens with more than two sexes, the item's cause (two gamete types) is in

### 5. Facultative adaptation and level of selection: two rules M-3 lacks

  - For **ENGINEERED** universals: creators may design for group or creator benefit (docility, cooperativeness,

### 8. Ch. 1's cautions, applied to claims about these races
**Brown basis:** pp. 9–38; p. 66 n. 9; p. 140. **M-3:** Step 9; HAR-HUF-41; future L4 work.

Read the list in both directions: it also warns against claims of *sameness* made on the same thin evidence.
- **A temporary condition read as permanent** (Tchambuli, p. 22). An early colony's crisis-era culture is not
  its species floor.
- **Ideology read as behavior** (Samoa; Goldschmidt n. 9). Norms vary more than behavior.
- **Emic testimony read as etic fact** (empathy, p. 140).
- **An incomplete corpus** (Hopi).
- **A universal structure whose strength varies read as absence** (Oedipus, p. 37).
- **A single founder population read as a species universal** (HAR-HUF-41; Brown on demonstration methods,
  pp. 51–52). A trait seen in one founding lineage is a lineage observation (M-3 Step 7) until other lineages
  confirm it.

"over-convergence" ("humans with ears") alarm.

### 9. Pseudospeciation becomes literal (M-4, M-6, HAR-HUF-10, HAR-HUF-37)
**Brown basis:** pp. 152–153.
- **The human analog.** Brown found that hereditary rank is justified by claiming rulers are a *different
  species*. Caste systems deny psychic unity, and open systems affirm it.
- **In this universe the species differences are real.** Creators and created are literally different species,
  and engineered-versus-born may be a real distinction.
- **Proposed:** treat "rank justified by species or origin difference" as a strong candidate for an
  **implicational universal** in mixed settlements (M-6): if rank is hereditary, then it is justified by
  claiming species-level difference. It is a condition-driven pattern, not an essence.
- **Rule 3 is sharpest here.** The setting must never *endorse* such a claim as explanation. It can only show
  peoples making it.
- **Designed minds** (p. 155 n. 5). Brown's note on "programmable" humans versus innate endowment bears directly
  on engineered temperament (HAR-HUF-14, HAR-HUF-36). Whether creators *could* make a race into "social
  automatons" is an M-4 question with an ethical edge. Raise it with the developer; don't decide it.

### 10. The Tiger-Fox "experimental Adam and Eve" is this repo's L0/L3 seam
**Brown basis:** p. 81; pp. 33–36 (kibbutz Oedipus); pp. 120–123 (kibbutz Westermarck).
- **The thought experiment, made real.** Tiger and Fox predict that people raised apart from culture would
  regenerate the universal pattern within a few generations. A lineage whose founders were reared apart from
  their creators' society is a real version of that experiment.
- **Brown's kibbutz evidence cuts both ways:**
  - collective rearing did **not** remove fixation on the mother (p. 35);
  - collective rearing **did** produce Westermarck inhibition among unrelated peers (p. 120).
  So rearing changes some attachment outcomes and not others.
- **Proposed:** L0 must record the founding rearing arrangement for each lineage: who reared the founders, and in
  what group sizes. Several L2 items (U-041, U-042, U-044, U-069, U-185, U-189) cannot be triaged without it.

### 11. Smaller flags tied to checklist items
- **U-033 to U-035 (time).** Brown roots time partly in biological clocks shared with "myriad other species"
  (p. 93). Activity cycle (P-1) and seasonal breeding (P-2) re-anchor the units. "Month" off Earth is still
  unanchored.
- **U-043 (snakes).** The human template is primate-shared (p. 115). Each base has its own predator template
  (verify). It is a candidate ANIMAL-INHERITED prepared fear, written as a preparedness (§4).
- **U-056 (fire).** Its utility rests on digestion, thermoregulation, and predator exposure (p. 95). If L1
  resolves digestion as human-dominant and adapted to cooked food, cooking dependence may transfer *directly*.
  Otherwise re-derive it from P-7 and P-9.
- **U-061 (group living).** Predator protection is primary for primates (pp. 111–112). For prey-base races the
  same root cause may hold more strongly. For a facultatively social base, check resource distribution (verify,
  M-2).
- **U-115 (sweets).** Brown frames this as a disposition that becomes a susceptibility in new environments
  (p. 111). For a race whose base lacks sweet taste (verify), the item is NON-TRANSFER. A designed sweet
  receptor would make it ENGINEERED.
- **U-116 and U-139 (religion, music).** Brown's partial explanations are *sensory*: infrasound, vestibular
  effects, percussion (pp. 114–116). A race with a different hearing range or vestibular system gets different
  "uncanny" triggers (verify; P-4). That is an ANALOG source of ritual form.
- **U-198 (playfighting, new).** Brown says it has innate bases with conditional expression (p. 148 n. 3).
  Predator-base and prey-base play may differ (verify). Write it as a drive per §4.
- **U-201 (the domestic dog, new).** For a dog-derived race, the human-dog relationship is a canon and ethics
  question for the developer. It is not a universal to transfer. Per rule 2, don't infer anything from the
  word "dog."

### 12. What M-3 should do differently (summary of proposals)
1. **Proximate-mechanism fields** (§1, Brown p. 129). For rearing- and kin-dependent items, Step 3 records the
   ultimate cause *and* the proximate mechanism: cue, sensitive window, channel. Step 4 checks each against L1.
2. **Split U-069** into avoidance (inhibition) and prohibition (taboo), with prevention handled in L1 (§1).
3. **Write ANIMAL-INHERITED as drives or preparedness**, not behaviors (§4).
4. **Add a facultative sub-check in Step 8.** Regular variation may be biological and conditional (§5).
5. **Require ENGINEERED items to state individual cost**, and flag costly ones for drift (§5).
6. **Walk the "too obvious" biology in Step 6** (§6).
7. **Add an over-divergence guard** beside the over-convergence guard (§8).
8. **Add an L0 rearing-arrangement field per lineage**, and log it as a question (§1, §10).

# Human Baseline (RQ-4)
**Status:** Working source reference, 2026-10-21. Real-world human values for M-1 Step 1. Reused for every hybrid race.
`Proposed:` until the developer accepts it (`CLAUDE.md` rule 6). Researched under LAW 0-R (rule 5); every value below
carries an `HB-S` source code. Paraphrase only; short quotes are marked.

---

## How to use

1. **This file is the "human baseline" column of M-1 Step 1.** For each of the 17 L1 domains, copy the relevant
   values *and the mechanism line* into the race's L1 draft next to the animal baseline (M-2) and creator intent (M-4).
   Resolution works on **mechanisms**, never surface behaviors (M-1 Step 2), so the mechanism line matters more
   than the number.
2. **Species-typical, with ranges.** Values are general human values, never tailored to any animal or race. Where
   a single number is given it is a central tendency; always carry the range and the variation notes with it.
3. **Confidence tags** (per value, in brackets):
   - `[Primary]` checked against the primary paper's own text or abstract in this pass.
   - `[Review]` from a peer-reviewed review or standard reference, checked at abstract level.
   - `[Secondary]` reached only through a secondary summary, press release, or citing paper. Usable as a lead;
     confirm before it becomes load-bearing.
   - `[Contested]` the literature disagrees; **both sides are recorded and M-1 must not pick one silently.**
4. **Variation is not identity.** Sex, age, and population differences below are *distributions that overlap*.
   Population frequencies (for example lactase persistence or color-vision deficiency) track ancestry, ecology,
   and history; they are not traits of a "kind of person" (`CLAUDE.md` rule 3; C-60). Never turn a population
   frequency into a character trait in any race document.
5. **Real world only.** Tepenian setting modifiers are kept in their own section and must not be folded into the
   values above them. M-1 uses the real-world baseline for mechanisms and applies setting modifiers separately.
6. **Cite as** `RQ-4 · Human Baseline, §Domain n` plus the `HB-S` code(s).

## Domains 1–17

### 1. Body plan, scale, and locomotion
- **Values & ranges.**
  - Stature: national adult means span roughly 20 cm between the tallest and shortest populations; men born in the Netherlands in the late 20th century averaged >182.5 cm; the shortest cohort in the dataset (Guatemalan women born 1896) averaged 140.3 cm. Some populations gained ~16–20 cm over the 20th century (South Korean women +20.2 cm, Iranian men +16.5 cm), others almost none `[Primary]` (HB-S27). Height is therefore strongly **environmentally plastic** (nutrition, disease load), not a fixed population constant.
  - Mass: world adult mean ~62 kg (WHO ~2005 data), regional means ~58 kg (Asia) to ~81 kg (Northern America) `[Primary]` (HB-S28).
  - Posture and locomotion: obligate habitual biped. Human walking costs ~75% less energy than chimpanzee walking (bipedal or quadrupedal), attributed to an extended hip and long hindlimb `[Primary]` (HB-S30). Preferred walking speed ~1.3–1.4 m/s in healthy adults under 60, falling to ~0.95 m/s at 80+ `[Review; per-decade values Secondary]` (HB-S31).
  - Running: humans are proposed to be specialized endurance runners (narrow pelvis, short toes, enlarged gluteus maximus attachment, large semicircular canals), dated to *Homo* ~2 Ma (HB-S29).
- **Mechanism.** Skeletal (pelvis, lumbar curve, long legs, arched foot), muscular (gluteal), vestibular (canals), plus thermoregulatory support for sustained running (see Domain 9).
- **Variation.** Sex: stature ~7% greater in men; mass 12–25% greater (HB-S32; see P-14). Age: walking speed declines after 60. Population: means differ, but the cited data show they move strongly with living conditions (HB-S27).
- **Contested.** Endurance-running-as-adaptation is disputed; critics argue persistence hunting was too rare and habitat-limited to drive evolution (HB-S29, via coverage). Record the anatomy as fact, the selective story as contested.
- **Sources.** HB-S27–S32.

### 2. Manipulation (hands, grip, fine motor control)
- **Values & ranges.** Fully opposable thumb; distinctive forceful precision grips (thumb and finger pads securing objects in one hand) plus power grips. Handedness: left-handed ~10.6% overall (10.4% excluding elite athletes); 9.3–18.1% depending on the definition used; ~9% use different hands for different tasks `[Review]` (HB-S34).
- **Mechanism.** Skeletal and muscular hand anatomy (Marzke identifies eight derived features supporting forceful precision grips; three already in *Australopithecus afarensis*, with limited thumb mobility) (HB-S33); dense fingertip mechanoreceptors feeding fast feedback control of grip force (HB-S24, Domain 6); cortical motor control. Hand laterality is neural (cerebral lateralization).
- **Variation.** Handedness prevalence varies with how it is measured, publication year, sex, and ancestry of samples (HB-S34). Grip strength differs by sex (HB-S32).
- **Contested.** Whether stone-tool making alone drove hand evolution: Marzke's 1997 work found the fine thumb-index pad grip was not tied to early toolmaking; chimpanzee feeding studies argue food processing also shaped grips (HB-S33).
- **Sources.** HB-S24, S32–S34.

### 3. Senses: vision
- **Values & ranges.**
  - Photoreceptors: ~92 million rods (77.9–107.3 M) and ~4.6 million cones (4.08–5.29 M) per retina; peak foveal cone density ~199,000/mm², very variable between people (100,000–324,000/mm²) `[Primary]` (HB-S01).
  - Visual field (monocular): ~100° temporal, ~60° nasal, ~60° up, ~75° down; the same reference also gives a narrower normal (~90° temporal, ~50° superior/nasal, ~60° inferior), so treat the limits as ±10° `[Review]` (HB-S02). Binocular overlap ~120°; combined horizontal field ~200° `[Secondary]` (HB-S03).
  - Acuity: Snellen 20/20 corresponds to a minimum angle of resolution of 1 arcminute and is the *lower limit of normal*, not perfection; many young adults see better (20/15, 20/10) `[Review]` (HB-S04). Acuity is roughly stable through midlife, declining moderately after ~60 (HB-S05). Near focus declines in middle age (presbyopia) (HB-S04).
  - Color: normal trichromacy, three cone classes peaking near ~420 nm (S), ~530–534 nm (M), ~560–564 nm (L), with individual variation in peaks; the CIE 2006 cone fundamentals are the standard model (HB-S06). Visible range roughly ~380/400–700 nm; the short-wave limit is set by lens and cornea absorption `[Secondary lead]` (HB-S06).
  - Scotopic (night) vision: rods peak ~498 nm (single-cell estimates ~491 nm), insensitive beyond ~640 nm; a dark-adapted rod is near a perfect photon counter, and a dark-adapted person can detect a handful of photons (HB-S09). Dark adaptation is slow and multi-stage: rod-cone break around ~10 minutes, most recovery within tens of minutes; exact times depend on bleach and method `[Secondary]` (HB-S09; see Gaps).
- **Mechanism.** Anatomical (fovea, lens, two receptor systems), photochemical (opsins), neural (opponent coding of cone signals). Humans are a **diurnal, fovea-dominant, color-rich** visual species with a usable but slow-adapting rod system.
- **Variation.**
  - Red-green color-vision deficiency is X-linked: up to ~8% of males and ~0.4–0.5% of females in populations of Northern European descent; lower reported rates in many Asian and African samples; individual studies vary widely with screening method (HB-S07).
  - Possible tetrachromacy in some female carriers of anomalous trichromacy (~12% of women carry it) remains weakly evidenced (HB-S08). `[Contested]`
  - Myopia is rising fast, linked to less time outdoors and more near work: projected ~50% of the world by 2050, high myopia ~10% (HB-S10). This is an environment-driven change, not a fixed trait.
- **Contested.** Tetrachromacy in humans (HB-S08). Exact field limits (HB-S02 internal inconsistency).
- **Sources.** HB-S01–S10.

### 4. Senses: hearing
- **Values & ranges.** About 20 Hz–20 kHz in healthy young listeners; the upper limit in average adults is often closer to 15–17 kHz `[Review]` (HB-S11). Greatest sensitivity in roughly the 2–5 kHz band, attributed to ear-canal resonance plus the middle-ear transfer function `[Secondary]` (HB-S13). Speech energy and the most informative speech cues fall in this sensitive band (inference; see Gaps).
- **Mechanism.** Anatomical (pinna, ear canal resonance, middle-ear ossicles), cochlear tonotopy (hair cells), neural binaural comparison for sound localization.
- **Variation.** Age-related high-frequency loss (presbycusis): significant loss at extended high frequencies (9–20 kHz) in older adults, but normative data are incomplete and vary widely across studies (HB-S12). Noise exposure is a major environmental modifier (general; not separately sourced here).
- **Contested.** None major; the precise sensitivity band edges differ by source (2–4 vs 2–5 kHz).
- **Sources.** HB-S11–S13.

### 5. Senses: smell and taste
- **Values & ranges (smell).**
  - **Revised model:** the claim that humans smell poorly traces to 19th-century anatomy (Broca's classification of humans as "nonsmellers" based on frontal-lobe expansion), not to sensory testing. McGann's review finds no anatomical basis for treating human olfaction as inferior; the human olfactory bulb is large with neuron counts comparable to other mammals; species differ mainly in *which* odorants they detect best `[Review]` (HB-S14). Treat "humans have a poor sense of smell" as an **outdated model** (log as a dead end, M-1 Guards).
  - Threshold comparisons: humans and spider monkeys showed no generalizable sensitivity difference for aldehydes despite large neuroanatomical and genetic differences; both detect many odorants below 1 ppm. Comparative threshold data across mammals remain thin (HB-S19).
  - Humans can scent-track: 21 of 32 blindfolded participants followed a 10 m chocolate-oil trail by smell, improved with practice, and used inter-nostril comparison (HB-S20).
  - Receptor genes: ~400 intact olfactory-receptor loci; 271 of 413 (66%) carry loss-of-function and/or copy-number variants in the population; an individual may carry up to ~600 receptor allelic variants. Each person has a **personalized receptor repertoire** (HB-S18). This is a mechanism for individual differences in what people can smell.
  - Number of discriminable odors: **unknown.** The 2014 "at least 1 trillion" estimate (HB-S15) was shown to rest on a fragile method that can produce values from ~4,500 to ~10²⁹, and the formula gives an upper, not lower, bound (HB-S16); a separate re-analysis reached the same conclusion and suggests odor-percept dimensionality may be ~20 or fewer (HB-S17). `[Contested]`
- **Values & ranges (taste).** Five widely recognized qualities: sweet and umami (TAS1R heterodimers), bitter (25 functional TAS2R genes), salty and sour (channel-type mechanisms) (HB-S22). Fat ("oleogustus") proposed as a sixth taste: panelists separated free-fatty-acid taste from bitter and umami; the authors' own wording is hedged ("may be") and it is typically unpleasant, signaling rancidity (HB-S23). Whether "basic tastes" is the right frame at all is debated (HB-S22). `[Contested]`
- **Mechanism.** G-protein-coupled receptor families (ORs, TAS1R, TAS2R), ion channels; olfactory bulb and cortex; retronasal smell contributes to flavor.
- **Variation.** Large individual variation from receptor-gene variation (HB-S18); bitter perception varies with TAS2R alleles (general; specific alleles not sourced here). Bitter tolerance is learnable (e.g., caffeine) (HB-S22).
- **Contested.** Odor-discrimination count (HB-S15–S17); fat as a basic taste (HB-S23); status of "basic tastes" (HB-S22).
- **Sources.** HB-S14–S23.

### 6. Senses: touch and others
- **Values & ranges.** Fingertip tactile afferents (four classes: SA-I, RA-I, SA-II, PC/Pacinian) signal contact, force direction, and object shape; the first spikes across an afferent population already carry force-direction and surface-shape information, and the brain plans manipulation around mechanical contact events (HB-S24). Classic two-point discrimination at the fingertip is ~2–3 mm, but the test itself is criticized as a poor acuity measure (subjects can exceed chance even at zero separation; large between-study variation) (HB-S25). Large ~250 Hz vibration mainly drives the Pacinian channel; non-Pacinian channels handle lower-frequency flutter `[Secondary]` (HB-S26).
- **Mechanism.** Cutaneous mechanoreceptors; proprioceptors; vestibular organs (enlarged semicircular canals, Domain 1); thermoreceptors and nociceptors. **No vibrissae** in humans; no specialized electro- or magnetoreception.
- **Variation.** Tactile acuity varies widely between people and declines with age (general; see Gaps).
- **Contested.** Validity of two-point discrimination (HB-S25).
- **Sources.** HB-S24–S26. Vestibular, proprioceptive, and interoceptive baselines are **not yet sourced** (Gaps).

### 7. Diet, dentition, and digestion
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Omnivore, highly variable.** Forager plant-to-animal ratios range widely: an ethnographic compilation found most (73%) forager societies got >50% of subsistence from animal foods (HB-S37, Cordain), but a 2023 re-examination of high-quality studies of tropical foragers found high variation, a temperature-linked gradient, and refuted fixed macronutrient ranges for "the" ancestral diet (HB-S37, Lieberman). `[Contested]` M-1 should treat human diet as **flexible omnivory**, not a single ratio.
  - **Cooking.** Strict long-term raw-food dieters lost ~10–12 kg on average; 15–25% were underweight and ~30% of women under 45 had partial or complete amenorrhea, more so at >90% raw (cross-sectional, self-reported) (HB-S36). The cooking hypothesis (cooking enabled *Homo erectus* brain and gut changes ~2 Ma) is contested: firm archaeological evidence of fire control is scarce before ~400 ka, though evidence is increasing; small jaws and teeth argue against a raw early-*Homo* diet (HB-S35). Record: humans today depend heavily on food processing; the *timing* of that dependence is contested.
  - **Dentition.** 32 permanent teeth (dental formula 2.1.2.3 per quadrant; 20 deciduous) — general anatomy; third molars are absent in ~22.6% of people worldwide (study range 5.3–56%), slightly more often in women (HB-S44).
  - **Gut.** The expensive-tissue hypothesis proposes a reduced gut offsetting the cost of a large brain (HB-S43). The brain is ~2% of body mass but uses ~20% of resting oxygen and calories (HB-S45).
  - **Obligate needs.** No vitamin C synthesis (GULO pseudogene, shared with anthropoid primates; losses appear irreversible) (HB-S41). No functional uricase: activity declined stepwise before pseudogenization; hominoid serum uric acid is several times higher than in most mammals (HB-S42).
- **Mechanism.** Dental (small teeth, weak jaws), digestive (gut proportions), enzymatic (lactase, amylase), and cultural (cooking, processing) — the last is learned, so it belongs partly to L3.
- **Variation.**
  - **Lactase persistence** is a population-genetic trait: from <10% non-persistence in some northern European samples to near-universal non-persistence in others; known variants do not fully explain the phenotype map in much of western and southern Africa, southeastern Europe, the Middle East, and parts of central and southern Asia (HB-S38). The widely cited global "two-thirds malabsorb" meta-analysis (HB-S39) is **retracted**; do not use it.
  - Salivary amylase gene (AMY1) copy number varies; the claim that it tracks starchy diets is contested on measurement and on amylase's actual role in starch digestion (HB-S40).
- **Contested.** Forager macronutrient ratios (HB-S37); cooking timing (HB-S35); expensive-tissue trade-off across mammals (HB-S43); AMY1 selection (HB-S40).
- **Sources.** HB-S35–S45.

### 8. Activity cycle and sleep
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Diurnal.** Intrinsic circadian period ~24.18 h, as stable and precise as other mammals', similar in young and older adults (HB-S52).
  - **Chronotypes:** near-Gaussian distribution of sleep timing in a population; chronotype depends on age and sex (HB-S53). In 53,689 US time-use diaries, chronotype became later through adolescence, peaking in lateness at ~19 years, then moved earlier; men were later than women before ~40 and earlier after (HB-S54).
  - **Light:** a week of natural light only (camping) shifted the clock ~2 h earlier and synchronized it to solar time; late types shifted most (HB-S55). Modern electric light is a major delaying environment.
  - **Sleep duration:** Hadza, San, and Tsimane averaged 5.7–7.1 h (pooled ~6.4 h), ~1 h more in winter, with onset ~3.3 h after sunset during falling night temperature; naps on <7% (winter) to <22% (summer) of days (HB-S56). Clinical consensus: adults 18–60 should regularly get ≥7 h (HB-S58); age-specific ranges: newborns 14–17 h, school-age 9–11 h, teens 8–10 h, adults 7–9 h, older adults 7–8 h (HB-S59).
  - **Group vigilance:** among Hadza, all members were simultaneously asleep for only 18 min over 20 days; chronotype differences that covary with age kept someone awake or lightly asleep in 99.8% of sampled epochs (HB-S57).
- **Mechanism.** Hypothalamic circadian clock entrained by light; homeostatic sleep pressure; temperature cycle as a natural regulator (HB-S56). Sleep architecture (NREM/REM cycling) is not yet sourced (Gaps).
- **Variation.** Age (newborn polyphasic sleep → adolescent delay → older-adult earlier and lighter sleep), sex (HB-S54), season (HB-S56), and light environment (HB-S55).
- **Contested.** **Segmented ("first and second") sleep**: historical texts suggest it was common in pre-industrial Western societies (HB-S60, Ekirch); critics read the terms differently and question source selection (HB-S60); the three-forager actigraphy study did not report a segmented pattern (HB-S56). Record as historically documented in some settings; universality contested.
- **Sources.** HB-S52–S60.

### 9. Thermoregulation and environmental tolerance
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Sweating:** eccrine gland density ~10× that of chimpanzees and macaques (HB-S61); functional density in 68 volunteers 61–133 glands/cm² across six sites, with sweat output only modestly related to density (gland-level output matters) (HB-S62). Sweat rates can exceed 1 L/h `[Secondary]` (HB-S61) and have been reported above 30 g/min (~1.8 kg/h) (HB-S63, 1996 stand); individual rates and sweat electrolytes vary a lot (HB-S63).
  - **Core temperature:** the 37.0 °C convention dates from Wunderlich (19th c.); US mean temperature has fallen ~0.03 °C per birth decade, and contemporary means are below 37.0 (HB-S65). Hypothermia: core <35 °C (HB-S67).
  - **Thermoneutral zone (nude, basal metabolism):** ~25.9–33.3 °C ambient; with ~1 clo clothing, ~14.5–24.5 °C (the paper is internally inconsistent on the lower bound) (HB-S66).
  - **Heat-humidity limit:** the theoretical 35 °C wet-bulb limit was not reached by any young, healthy subject; measured critical wet-bulb temperatures averaged ~30.6 °C in warm-humid conditions and lower (~25–28 °C) in hot-dry ones at modest activity (HB-S64).
  - **Light/UV:** skin pigmentation is a clinal adaptation balancing UV protection (folate) against vitamin D synthesis; depigmentation and tanning evolved several times independently (HB-S68).
  - **Hypoxia:** Andean, Tibetan, and Ethiopian Amhara highlanders adapted along different physiological routes (Andeans with elevated hemoglobin; Tibetans and Amhara without); EPAS1 and EGLN1 show selection in Tibetans (HB-S69). The highest permanent city (La Rinconada, 5,100–5,300 m) shows 44% excessive erythrocytosis and 14% chronic mountain sickness among mostly short-term residents (HB-S70).
  - **Acceleration and gravity:** unprotected healthy men can lose consciousness near +4 Gz with rapid onset; trained pilots with suits and straining are tested to ~7 G for 15 s (HB-S71). Microgravity: bone loss ~1–1.5% per month (meta-analysis: lower-limb ~0.8%/month), site-dependent (HB-S72).
- **Mechanism.** Evolutionary: EN1-regulated eccrine density, reduced body hair (HB-S61); physiological: sweating, vasodilation, shivering and brown-fat thermogenesis; behavioral and cultural: clothing, shelter, fire (outside L1).
- **Variation.** Large individual variation in sweat rate (HB-S63) and tolerance (HB-S64); heat acclimatization (general); population-level genetic adaptations to altitude and UV (HB-S68, S69) — population frequencies, not character traits.
- **Contested.** Universality of the 35 °C limit (HB-S64); thermoneutral bounds depend on skin and core temperature, not air temperature alone (HB-S66).
- **Sources.** HB-S61–S72.

### 10. Reproduction (cycle type, mating system, gestation, litter)
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Cycle type:** spontaneous ovulation; continuous (non-seasonal) breeding; menstrual cycle. In 612,613 ovulatory cycles (app users, mainly Sweden, UK, USA): mean 29.3 days; only ~13% exactly 28 days; 65% of women had cycles of 25–30 days; follicular phase 16.9 d (95% range 10–30), luteal 12.4 d (7–17); follicular phase shortens ~0.19 d per year from 25 to 45 (HB-S73). Not a random population sample.
  - **Gestation:** ~268 days from ovulation to birth (~38 weeks 2 days); natural range ~37 days even after excluding preterm births; only ~4% deliver on the 280-day due date; a woman's pregnancies tend to be similar in length (n = 125) (HB-S74).
  - **Litter:** singleton births are the norm. Twinning averages 13.6 per 1,000 births across 76 low- and middle-income countries; Central Africa >18 per 1,000; Asia and Latin America often <8–10 per 1,000 (HB-S75). Twinning rose substantially in Europe, North America, and Asia between 1980–85 and 2010–15 (Asia +32%, North America +71%); Africa had the highest rates in both periods with no significant rise (HB-S75). The cause of the rise was not checked in this pass.
  - **Interbirth interval (natural fertility):** forager mean ~3.1 years (2.8 Agta – 3.3 Hiwi); age at first birth just under ~20; age at last birth 36–42 (median ~38); mean total fertility ~6.2 (HB-S82, secondary summary). Human IBIs are significantly shorter than wild chimpanzees' (3.6 ± 0.3 y) (HB-S82). TFR ranges from ~4.7 (!Kung) to 8+ (Ache; up to 9.2 Tsimane) (HB-S82).
  - **Generation time:** mean ~26.9 ± 3.4 years over 250,000 years (fathers ~30.7, mothers ~23.2); recent maternal ~26.4 (HB-S83). Feeds P-5.
- **Ovulation signaling.** Usually described as "concealed" (no conspicuous swelling). Evidence suggests subtle cues near ovulation (clothing choice, voice pitch, body-odor ratings) (HB-S76). Why concealment evolved has no consensus; an agent-based model favored a female-rivalry explanation over the male-investment one (HB-S76). Whether women's mate preferences shift across the cycle: two 2014 meta-analyses reached opposite conclusions, followed by replication failures (HB-S77). `[Contested]`
- **Mating system.** No single answer. ~85% of societies in the ethnographic record permitted polygyny (HB-S78), but within any society most marriages are monogamous; pair bonds exist in all societies, most often as serial monogamy (HB-S80). Coding critique: many "limited polygyny" societies (<20% polygynous marriages) could as easily be coded mostly monogamous (HB-S78). Polyandry is rare but found worldwide in 53 societies outside the classic Himalayan cases, often in egalitarian groups with male-biased sex ratios or high male mortality (HB-S79). Extra-pair paternity is low: contemporary ~1–2%, historically low in Western and African genealogical data, with local variation (<0.5% to ~6%) (HB-S81). Sexual dimorphism and relative testis size suggest humans diverged from the great apes in sexual-selection history; the timing of the anatomical changes is debated (HB-S80).
- **Mechanism.** Hormonal (HPG axis; lactational suppression of ovulation spaces births in natural-fertility settings — general physiology, not separately sourced here); anatomical (single uterus, one offspring per pregnancy typical); behavioral pair-bonding and marriage (L3 rules sit on top).
- **Variation.** Cycle length varies with age and BMI (HB-S73); twinning with population and maternal age (HB-S75); fertility with energy balance and lactation (HB-S82).
- **Contested.** Concealed ovulation (HB-S76); cycle shifts (HB-S77); "the" human mating system (HB-S78, S80).
- **Sources.** HB-S73–S83.

### 11. Development and maturation
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Neonatal state:** "secondarily altricial" (Portmann): born relatively immature. Neonatal brain ~25–30% of adult size in humans vs ~33–43% in other primates (sources differ: ~28% vs ~43% primate average) (HB-S84). Across 140 placental mammals, humans became more altricial fastest, mainly through very large *postnatal* brain growth (HB-S84). The brain reaches ~80% of maximum size by ~3 years; cortical gray matter peaks ~5.9 y, subcortical ~14.4 y, white matter ~28.7 y (HB-S90).
  - **Weaning:** average breastfeeding ~29 months (SD 10) in 113 nonindustrial populations; non-milk liquids ~4.5 months, solids ~5.0 months (HB-S86).
  - **Life stages:** infancy, childhood (from weaning to roughly first permanent molar), juvenile, adolescence (growth spurt starting ~10 in girls and ~12 in boys; skeletal growth ending ~18–19 in girls and ~20–22 in boys), adulthood (HB-S85).
  - **Long dependency:** no forager children produced as much food as they consumed until their mid-to-late teens; by 15 they had consumed >25% of lifetime energy but produced <5% (HB-S87).
  - **Puberty timing:** US median menarche 12.43 y, 90% by 13.75 y (NHANES III) (HB-S89). Breast-development onset has moved earlier by ~0.24 years per decade (1977–2019), earliest in US samples, latest in African samples (HB-S89).
- **Mechanism.** Developmental (extended postnatal brain growth, delayed maturation), endocrine (puberty), energetic (provisioning by others).
- **Who rears young.** Mothers plus fathers, grandmothers, siblings, and others: allocare is central to human demographic success (HB-S88). Whether this makes humans "cooperative breeders" in the classical sense is debated ("biocultural reproduction" alternative) (HB-S88). `[Contested]`
- **Variation.** Puberty timing shifts with nutrition and secular trend and differs by population (HB-S89); weaning age varies widely and partly with subsistence (earlier with dairy and cereal foods for some measures) (HB-S86).
- **Contested.** **Obstetric dilemma vs metabolic (EGG) hypothesis**: the classic view says a narrow bipedal birth canal truncated gestation; Dunsworth et al. argue maternal metabolic limits set gestation length (HB-S84). Unsettled. Whether childhood and adolescence are new stages (Bogin) or delays of existing ones is also debated (HB-S85).
- **Sources.** HB-S84–S90.

### 12. Lifespan and aging
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Life expectancy ≠ lifespan.** Life expectancy at birth (e0) is an average pulled down by infant and child deaths; lifespan describes how long bodies last once early hazards are survived.
  - **Foragers:** e0 21–37 years among traditional hunter-gatherers; 57% survive to 15; of those, ~64% reach 45; expected remaining life at 45 ~20 years (14–24). Mortality falls through childhood, stays roughly flat to ~40, then rises in Gompertz fashion. **Modal adult age at death ~72 (range 68–78)**; the authors propose human bodies are built to function well for about seven decades (HB-S91) `[Primary, text checked]`.
  - **Modern:** global e0 ~73.2–73.3 years (2023) `[Secondary]` (HB-S92). Sex gap not verified this pass (Gaps).
  - **Maximum:** the 122-year record (Calment) is the accepted validated maximum but has been challenged (HB-S93). A proposed hard limit (~115) was criticized as driven by one data point and weak statistics (HB-S93). `[Contested]`
  - **Menopause:** mean age at natural menopause 48.8 y (95% CI 48.3–49.2) across 46 studies in 24 countries; lowest estimates in African, Latin American, Asian, and Middle Eastern samples, highest in Europe and Australia (HB-S94). A long post-reproductive life is typical even in forager demography (HB-S91).
  - Menopause outside humans occurs in five toothed-whale species, apparently evolved independently (HB-S96).
- **Mechanism.** Senescence (mortality-rate doubling ~7–8 years in human populations, HB-S91); ovarian follicle depletion for menopause (general).
- **Variation.** Sex, socioeconomic and environmental conditions strongly shape e0; population means for menopause differ but overlap (HB-S94).
- **Contested.** **Why menopause and long post-reproductive life evolved**: grandmother hypothesis (HB-S95) — support is mixed (maternal grandmothers often help child survival; meta-analysis in patrilineal populations fit local resource competition better; some historical data show negative effects) (HB-S95); alternatives include two-sex/biparental-investment and embodied-capital accounts (HB-S87, S95).
- **Sources.** HB-S87, S91–S96.

### 13. Communication channels
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Language in all societies**, in speech or sign. Sign languages are full languages: children in Nicaragua created hierarchical, segmented linguistic structure within ~25 years from gesture (HB-S101).
  - **Vocal anatomy:** humans lost the laryngeal air sacs and vocal membranes present in other primates, giving a stable, less chaotic voice source suited to speech (43 primate species) (HB-S98). The descended larynx is no longer considered uniquely necessary for speech: laryngeal descent occurs in other species, and monkeys produce contrasting formant patterns; the decisive human difference is articulator and laryngeal *control* (HB-S97). Vocal learning is rare in mammals and constrained in other primates (HB-S103).
  - **Pitch:** adult male voices average about an octave below adult female voices; human F0 dimorphism exceeds every other ape's, driven by pubertal testosterone thickening the vocal folds (HB-S99). Typical speaking-F0 figures (men ~85–155 Hz, women ~165–255 Hz) are from non-peer-reviewed summaries; one Korean daily-speech dataset gave medians of 114 Hz (men) and 199 Hz (women) `[Secondary]` (see Gaps).
  - **Information rate:** across 17 languages, speech rate and information per syllable trade off, giving ~39 bits/s (HB-S100).
  - **Body and face signals:** high-contrast white sclera is proposed to aid gaze-following (HB-S102), but comparative work finds a continuum of eye pigmentation among hominoids and individual variation in gorillas (HB-S102). `[Contested]` Facial and vocal emotion signals: see Domain 14.
  - **Chemical signals:** no human pheromone has been demonstrated by bioassay; the four steroids often marketed as human pheromones lack robust evidence (HB-S21). Body odor carries information that people can rate (HB-S76) — an olfactory cue, not a demonstrated pheromone.
- **Mechanism.** Anatomical (simplified larynx, tongue, lips), neural (direct neocortical connections to phonatory motor neurons are reported as distinctive in humans — secondary, via a review of Fitch's earlier book; unverified), learned (language is acquired, Domain 15 enculturation).
- **Signal control (P-8 extension).** Speech and sign: voluntary and learned. Gaze direction: voluntary, but sclera contrast is anatomical. Voice pitch baseline: involuntary anatomy, but trainable within range. Body odor: involuntary.
- **Variation.** Sex (F0), age (voice change at puberty), language (rate) (HB-S99, S100).
- **Sources.** HB-S21, S76, S97–S103.

### 14. Emotion expression
- **Values & ranges.** Humans express emotion through face, voice (including nonverbal vocalizations such as laughs and screams), posture, and touch. Facial movement uses two partly separate control routes: voluntary (motor cortex, pyramidal) and spontaneous (subcortical, extrapyramidal); upper-face muscles (e.g., orbicularis oculi in the "Duchenne" smile) are harder to move voluntarily than the lower face `[Secondary]` (HB-S110).
- **Contested: universality of basic emotions.** Record both sides; M-1 must not assume either.
  - **Universality side:** emotion recognition from faces exceeds chance across cultures (HB-S107); 16 facial-expression types occurred in similar contexts across 6 million videos from 144 countries, with ~70% shared context associations (the 70% figure is from press materials, `[Secondary]`) (HB-S105); nonverbal vocalizations for basic emotions were recognized in both directions between British and remote Namibian (Himba) listeners (HB-S109).
  - **Cultural-specificity / constructionist side:** recognition is better within one's own group (in-group advantage), smaller with more intergroup contact (HB-S107, though Matsumoto disputes it); Western participants represented the six "basic" emotions with distinct facial movement sets while East Asian participants did not (HB-S106); a review of >1,000 studies concluded that the same configuration can signal different emotions by context and that emotion cannot be reliably read off faces (HB-S104); meta-analysis found basic emotions co-occur with their predicted faces only weakly (average effect ~0.13 for full expressions, ~0.23 including partial; r ~0.30 for intensity) (HB-S108); a Himba re-test argued vocalizations signal valence rather than specific emotions, and the original authors conceded that three of four positive emotions were not reliably recognized (HB-S109).
  - **Working reading for M-1:** humans have species-typical expressive *machinery* (facial musculature, vocal production, dual control pathways) and some cross-culturally shared expression-context links; mapping of specific configurations to specific emotions is substantially shaped by context and culture. This is a summary of the debate, not a resolution of it.
- **Mechanism.** Anatomical (mimetic muscles, larynx), neural (dual pathways), learned (display rules, L3).
- **Signal control (P-8).** Spontaneous expressions partly involuntary; posed expressions voluntary; display rules trainable.
- **Variation.** Individual and cultural; avoid any claim that a population is "more" or "less" expressive.
- **Sources.** HB-S104–S110.

### 15. Cognition
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Brain:** ~86 billion neurons (86.1 ± 8.1 billion) and a similar number of non-neuronal cells; the human brain is a scaled-up primate brain in cellular terms (HB-S46). Energy: ~2% of body mass, ~20% of resting energy (HB-S45).
  - **Working memory:** focus-of-attention capacity ~3–5 chunks (about 4), not Miller's 7 ± 2; estimates still debated (HB-S48).
  - **Social cognition:** 2.5-year-old children matched chimpanzees and orangutans on physical-world tasks but outperformed both on social-learning, communication, and theory-of-mind tasks (cultural intelligence hypothesis) (HB-S47). Explicit false-belief understanding emerges around 4–5 years; a standardized test across Canada, India, Peru, Samoa, and Thailand found onset at ~5 years in all five (HB-S49).
  - **Enculturation (P-12; detailed here because the template has no separate domain):**
    - *Language sensitive period:* grammar-learning ability stays high until ~17.4 years and then declines steadily, from 669,498 speakers (HB-S122) — later than earlier speculation (puberty; Lenneberg) (HB-S123). Late first-language exposure (deaf children without early sign input) produces larger and more pervasive deficits than late second-language learning, affecting all later language learning and reading (HB-S124). Phonological categories are tuned in the first year (HB-S124).
    - *Social learning:* children copy even visibly unnecessary actions (overimitation) where chimpanzees drop them (HB-S125); why (social affiliation vs causal belief) is debated (HB-S125).
    - *Teaching:* traditionally described as rare in small-scale societies, where learning is mostly observation and participation; Aka forager infancy shows subtle teaching (HB-S126). `[Contested]`
    - *Transmission:* forager social learning is reported as primarily vertical (parent to child) in one analysis (HB-S126); large networks of unrelated co-residents support cumulative culture (HB-S114).
- **Mechanism.** Neural (scale, cortical expansion), developmental (long sensitive periods, Domain 11), social (shared attention, imitation, pedagogy).
- **Variation.** Most psychology data come from WEIRD samples (one 2003–07 count: 96% of subjects; 68% US) and those samples are often outliers on perception, fairness, cooperation, and spatial reasoning (HB-S50). **Treat any "typical human cognition" value as WEIRD-biased unless shown cross-culturally.** Sex differences: across 106 meta-analyses (12 million people), the mean psychological sex difference is small (d ≈ 0.21; 85% of effects small or very small) (HB-S51).
- **Contested.** Working-memory number (HB-S48); overimitation causes (HB-S125); teaching (HB-S126).
- **Sources.** HB-S45–S51, S114, S122–S126.

### 16. Social instinct
- **Values & ranges.**
  - **Group structure: multilevel.** Social group sizes form a discrete hierarchy with a ratio of ~3 between layers (~3–5, 9–15, 30–45, …) (HB-S113); in 339 forager societies the scaling ratio is close to 4 (HB-S113). Co-residing forager bands: mean experienced band size 28.2 adults across 32 societies (HB-S114).
  - **"Dunbar's number" (~150) — contested.** For: extrapolating the primate neocortex–group-size regression gives ~148 (95% CI ~100–230) (HB-S111); Dunbar points to recurrence of ~150 in many settings (HB-S112). Against: re-running the method on updated primate data with Bayesian and phylogenetic methods gave means of 69–109 and 16–42, with 95% intervals of 4–520 and 2–336, so no single cognitive limit can be derived this way (HB-S112). Dunbar replied that the critique uses flawed statistics (HB-S112). Record both; P-3 should carry a range, not 150.
  - **Kinship and kin recognition:** most band members are genetically unrelated; adult brothers and sisters often co-reside (HB-S114). Humans use cues — seeing one's mother care for an infant, and years of childhood co-residence — to estimate relatedness, which regulates sibling altruism and incest aversion (HB-S118).
  - **Dominance and status:** forager egalitarianism is maintained by coalitions that block would-be dominants ("reverse dominance") (HB-S119); status comes through dominance (coercion) or prestige (freely given deference to skilled people) (HB-S119).
  - **Territoriality:** variable and ecology-dependent; models disagree on whether territoriality is greatest where resources are dense and predictable or sparse and variable (HB-S121). Home ranges grow less than proportionally with group size (HB-S113, Hamilton PNAS).
  - **Lethal aggression:** in 21 mobile forager band societies, most lethal events were homicides, some feud, a minority war (HB-S120); others estimate ~14% of forager deaths from intergroup conflict, a figure since revised down (~9%) by a critique (HB-S120). `[Contested]`
- **Dispersal/philopatry (P-13; template places it here).**
  - Foragers: **either sex may disperse or stay** (HB-S114); foragers are more multilocal/bilocal than non-foragers (HB-S115). One forager table: ~51% patri/virilocal, ~15% matri/uxorilocal, ~34% other (Kelly-based, HB-S116).
  - All societies in the Ethnographic Atlas: patrilocal is most common (~65–71% depending on coding), matrilocal ~11–20%, the two together ~85% of cases (HB-S116). These numbers depend on coding rules and sample; they describe **social residence rules**, which are cultural (L3), not an instinct.
  - Genetics: early work found greater Y-chromosome than mtDNA differentiation, read as higher female migration under patrilocality (HB-S117); later work shows the signal depends on scale (regional, not necessarily global) and is smaller than first thought, with regional variation (HB-S117). `[Contested]`
- **Mechanism.** Neural and hormonal affiliation systems (not sourced in detail here), kin-detection cues (HB-S118), and above all cultural institutions (marriage, residence rules) that sit in L3.
- **Variation.** Very large cross-cultural variation; any "human social structure" claim should name the subsistence context.
- **Sources.** HB-S111–S121.

### 17. Health and vulnerabilities
- **Values & ranges (species-level vulnerabilities, with mechanism).**
  - Dietary: vitamin C required (GULO loss, HB-S41); high uric acid (uricase loss, HB-S42); lactose malabsorption in adults where lactase does not persist (HB-S38).
  - Musculoskeletal: low back pain is the leading global cause of years lived with disability, >0.5 billion prevalent cases in 2020, projected >800 million by 2050 (HB-S127). Commonly linked in the mismatch literature to upright posture and modern life (HB-S128) — the evolutionary link is an interpretation, not shown in HB-S127.
  - Visual: myopia epidemic tied to near work and little outdoor time (HB-S10); X-linked color-vision deficiency (HB-S07).
  - Heat: wet-bulb limits below theory (HB-S64); hypothermia below 35 °C core (HB-S67).
  - Hypoxia: chronic mountain sickness and excessive erythrocytosis at extreme altitude (HB-S70).
  - Gravity: G-LOC near +4 Gz unprotected (HB-S71); microgravity bone loss (HB-S72).
  - Reproductive: difficult birth (obstetric dilemma, contested cause, HB-S84); long helpless infancy (Domain 11).
  - Dental: third-molar crowding and agenesis variation (HB-S44); cavities treated as a mismatch symptom (HB-S128).
  - Diet change: long-term strict raw diets reduce weight and menstrual function (HB-S36).
- **Mechanism categories.** Mismatch (fast environmental change vs slow selection), constraint, and design trade-off (HB-S128).
- **Variation.** Population-specific genetic frequencies (lactase, color vision, altitude adaptation) — distributions, not identities.
- **Sources.** HB-S07, S10, S36, S38, S41, S42, S44, S64, S67, S70–S72, S84, S127, S128.

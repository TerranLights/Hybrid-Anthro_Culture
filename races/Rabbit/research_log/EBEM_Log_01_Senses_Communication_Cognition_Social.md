# EBEM Research Log 01 — Senses, Communication, Cognition, Social (O. cuniculus)
**Status:** M-2 Steps A–B, 2026-10-21. Step C (retention against L1) pending M-1.

**Scope of this log:** L1 domains 3–6 (senses), 13 (communication), 14 (emotion expression), 15 (cognition),
16 (social instinct), plus predator vigilance and anti-predator behavior, and play. Body, life history,
reproduction, diet, activity, thermoregulation, and health are covered by a separate log and appear here only
as context. Everything below is `Proposed:` input data (CLAUDE.md rule 6). No race trait is written here.

**Step 0 compliance notes.**
- **LAW 0-R.** Every fact below was retrieved during this session. Recalled facts are marked
  `(recalled, unverified)` where one is used for orientation only. Dead ends are logged in the Search log.
- **Coordinate, never a cause.** Study sites, breeds, lab strains, and introduction histories appear only as
  coordinates (Step F field). No sentence below makes a population's human context the cause of a trait.
  Domestication findings are recorded as *mechanisms of change* (brain, genes). Per the sequencing rule they
  become causal only if a lineage charter names domestic stock.
- **Folk meaning.** None used. One owner-survey source (S-17) uses a pet-culture term; it is quoted as the
  source's term and weighted as owner-reported.
- **Role nouns.** None imported. "Dominant/subordinate" and "rank" are the sources' operational terms for
  outcomes of agonistic interactions, not roles.

**Access legend** (how much of each source was actually read; this sets the ceiling on confidence):
`[FT]` full text read · `[AB]` abstract read directly (PubMed/Crossref/OpenAlex/publisher) · `[SN]` abstract or
findings seen only via a search-engine summary or a citing paper (secondary; treat numbers as provisional) ·
`[TI]` title/metadata only (existence verified, content not) · `[LEAD]` non-peer-reviewed lead.

**Management-status tags** used in the inventory: `wild` (free-living, native or introduced), `wild-encl`
(wild-origin animals in a large semi-natural field enclosure), `wild-pen` (wild-type animals in outdoor pens),
`feral`, `domestic`, `lab` (laboratory strain, usually New Zealand White), `captive` (other captivity).

**Step A hypothesis check (RWBEM: "lower tiers are often the most valuable").** It holds here, strongly. Almost
all sensory data come from the Supporting tier (lab rabbits, mostly albino New Zealand White). Almost all
social-structure data come from the Secondary tier: introduced populations (Australia, Britain, New Zealand)
and a wild-origin German field-enclosure colony. The Primary tier (native Iberian range) contributed only
predator-stress, territorial-odor, and anti-predator studies (S-43, S-60, S-65). No native-range study of
group composition was found (see gaps).

## Inventory by L1 domain

Confidence: **High** = consistent primary data read at [FT]/[AB], ideally replicated; **Medium** = one
primary study read at [AB], or replicated but [SN]; **Low** = [SN]/[TI]/[LEAD] or single small-n study.

### 3 · Senses: vision

- **Two cone classes (dichromacy) plus rods.** Short-wavelength ("blue") cones peak near 425 nm,
  middle-wavelength ("green") cones near 523 nm, rods near 498 nm, from increment-threshold spectral
  sensitivity (S-01 [SN]). Behavioral spectral discrimination was shown in 1971 (S-02 [TI]). Visual-cortex
  neurons build a four-dimensional color-plus-brightness space (two chromatic axes), consistent with
  dichromacy (S-09 [AB], `lab`). **Mechanism:** two cone opsins; color opponency in cortex.
  *Confidence: Medium* (peak values are [SN]; the two-cone finding is consistent across S-01, S-03, S-09).
- **Uneven retinal sampling: a horizontal visual streak.** Cone density peaks (~18,000/mm²) in the middle of a
  horizontal band; dorsal retina ~7,000/mm²; ventral plateau 10,000–12,000/mm² (S-03 [SN], `lab` NZW). Rod
  density peaks just dorsal to the streak (S-03). **Mechanism → brain:** the superior colliculus magnifies
  the horizon: the band 15° above and below the horizontal meridian takes up more than half of the 100° of
  field visible on the colliculus surface, and the upper and lower 60° get only a small strip (S-04 [FT],
  `domestic`, n=7). Optokinetic responses are driven most by central (streak) stimulation (S-08 [SN]).
  *Confidence: High* for the horizon-weighted streak; Medium for the numbers.
- **Short-wavelength cones are concentrated in a far-ventral band.** OS-2-labeled S-cones make up essentially
  all photoreceptors (~13,000/mm²) in a horizontal band near the ventral edge (original finding attributed to
  Juliusson and colleagues), while S-cone density is only ~1,000–1,500/mm² elsewhere (S-03 [SN]). *Inference
  (not stated by the source):* the ventral retina images the upper visual field, so the S-cone-rich band
  samples the sky. *Confidence: Medium* (finding); the sky interpretation is an untested inference.
- **Field of view: near-panoramic, monocular-dominated.** The eyes are directed more laterally than in most
  mammals, giving "a visual field of almost 360°" (S-06 [LEAD]). Each contralateral superior colliculus
  represents the full 180° of its eye's field; in the striate cortex the temporal 168° are represented
  contralaterally and the most nasal 12° ipsilaterally (S-04 [FT]), so a narrow frontal strip is shared by both
  eyes. Rabbits can solve visual discriminations that need binocular information (S-07 [AB]).
  **Binocular overlap in degrees: UNRESOLVED.** Figures circulating online (10–30°; ~20°; 27–32° attributed to
  a 1942 source) could not be traced to a readable primary source (Heesy 2004 table blocked; Hughes 1971 [TI]).
  Blind areas (front/behind/under the muzzle) are likewise unverified. *Confidence: Medium* (near-panoramic,
  narrow binocular strip); *Unresolved* (overlap value, blind areas).
- **Acuity: UNRESOLVED.** No behavioral acuity value was retrieved. The photoreceptor-topography paper notes
  that published behavioral acuity in rabbits is lower than its anatomy predicts (S-03 [SN]). OKN work used
  0.05–0.5 cycles/degree gratings but the snippet gave no threshold (S-08 [SN]).
- **Light sensitivity.** Rods predominate; no tapetum lucidum (S-06 [LEAD]). *Confidence: Low–Medium.*
- **Development.** Eyelids open on postnatal days (PND) 10–12; ocular histology nearly mature by PND 28–42
  (S-10 [AB], `lab` albino). Orienting (head and pinna) responses to a tone are absent before PND 10
  (S-16 [AB]). *Confidence: High* (for lab white rabbits).
- **Population/condition caveat.** Most retinal data come from albino New Zealand White rabbits. Albinism is a
  known retinal-development confound in mammals `(recalled, unverified)`; wild pigmented-rabbit data were not
  found. Record as a gap, not a trait.

### 4 · Senses: hearing

- **Range.** Behavioral audiogram (domestic): high-frequency limit 49 kHz at 60 dB SPL (S-11 [AB]). The
  low-frequency limit (~96 Hz at 60 dB) was seen only in a secondary table summary (S-11 [SN]),
  **unverified**. For context only, a different genus (cottontail) hears 300 Hz–32 kHz at 60 dB (S-15,
  contrast, never a substitute). *Confidence: High* (upper limit); Low (lower limit).
- **Sound localization is coarse, and this is coupled to vision.** Rabbit left/right localization thresholds
  are ~22° (cited in S-13 [AB]); ITD discrimination threshold 50–60 µs for reference ITDs 0–±200 µs
  (S-13, `lab`). Across species, localization acuity correlates negatively with the width of the field of
  best vision; hearing's job is to point the eyes and attention, so a broad horizontal streak needs only
  coarse localization (S-12 [AB], `domestic`; S-15 contrast). **Mechanism:** audition → reflexive orienting
  of a wide-streak visual system. *Confidence: High* (pattern), Medium (exact rabbit thresholds).
- **The pinna is the main spectral and high-frequency directional filter.** Head-related transfer functions
  (n=9): broad gain peak at 2.6 kHz (+12–23 dB by azimuth); notch at 7.6 kHz; peak at 9.8 kHz; interaural level
  differences 5–16 kHz span ±31 dB. Removing the pinnae removed all spectral peaks/notches and most
  high-frequency ILD (S-14 [AB], `lab`). **Mechanism:** large external pinnae as acoustic horns.
  *Confidence: High.*
- **Ear mobility.** Orienting responses include head and pinna movement (from PND ~10; S-16). The range,
  independence, and musculature of pinna movement were **not** found in a primary source. *Unresolved.*
- **Conformation variation (domestic).** In an owner survey, lop-eared and half-lop rabbits had more
  vet-indicated ear conditions (~25% vs ~10% erect-eared); rabbits with ear problems were reported less
  responsive to relevant sounds (S-17 [AB], owner-reported). *Confidence: Low–Medium.* Coordinate only
  until a charter names lop stock.

### 5 · Senses: smell and taste

- **Large functional olfactory-receptor repertoire.** The rabbit reference genome carries 768 intact OR genes,
  22 truncated, 256 pseudogenes (24.5% pseudogenes) (S-18 [FT, Fig. 1A]). For scale only, the same figure
  lists 396 intact for human (human baseline belongs to RQ-4, not here). **Caveat:** gene count is not
  behavioral acuity; no adult olfactory-threshold study was found. *Confidence: High* (count).
- **Neonatal chemical signal detected by the main olfactory system, not the VNO.** Lactating females emit
  2-methylbut-2-enal (the "mammary pheromone", MP), which releases stereotyped nipple search and grasping
  (S-19 [AB]). VNO removal did not affect pups' response; irrigating the nasal mucosa with ZnSO₄ abolished it
  (S-21 [AB]). **Mechanism:** main olfactory epithelium → main olfactory bulb. *Confidence: High.*
- **The MP is also a teacher.** It acts as an unconditioned stimulus that makes pups learn a novel odor in one
  pairing, with memory needing consolidation/reconsolidation (S-20 [AB]). Rapid suckling-odor learning
  declines markedly by PND 5 (S-22 [AB]). Fetuses learn dietary odors in utero and are attracted to them as
  neonates (S-23 [AB]). **Mechanism:** a developmentally limited, pheromone-gated learning window.
  *Confidence: High* (`lab/domestic`).
- **VNO present and anatomically equipped for active sampling.** Bilateral organ at the ventral nasal septum;
  double capsule (bony outside, cartilaginous inside) and well-developed venous sinuses suggest a pumping
  mechanism; indirect connection to the mouth via a functional nasopalatine duct (S-24 [AB]). VNO gene
  expression is plastic: sex separation shifts receptor expression, in opposite directions in adult vs
  juvenile females (S-25 [AB]). The vomeronasal pathway is sexually dimorphic (accessory bulb female > male in
  some cell counts; medial amygdala male > female) (S-26 [AB]). **Gap:** no experiment in my sources shows
  what the VNO does in adult rabbit social behavior. *Confidence: High* (anatomy), *Unresolved* (function).
- **Scent glands available as signal sources:** submandibular (chin), anal, inguinal, Harderian, lacrimal,
  preputial, and the mammary–nipple complex (S-27 [AB], S-28 [FT]). Details under 13.
- **Taste.** Sugar preference thresholds (n=12): maltose 30 mM; polycose and sucrose 50 mM; glucose 75 mM;
  fructose 150 mM; lactose 175 mM; relative preference maltose = polycose > sucrose > glucose > fructose ≥
  lactose, interpreted as a possible starch-sensitive taste channel (S-29 [AB]). Taste-bud counts: wild-living
  rabbits ~43% and domestic ~25% fewer than the hare; domestic > wild; ~96% of buds on the posterior tongue
  (S-30 [AB]). **Variation:** domestic vs wild taste-bud number differs (record both).
  *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Learned diet flavors** (vertical transmission): see 15.

### 6 · Senses: touch and others

- **The face dominates the touch map.** In primary somatosensory cortex, circumoral surfaces (lips, philtrum,
  nose, vibrissae) occupy ~86.4% of the map; the pinna has its own displaced representation (S-31 [AB],
  `lab`). Whisker barrels are mapped in awake rabbits (Song 2010, PubMed 20171289 [AB]). Whisker movements
  were filmed in domestic rabbits for the first time in 2023 (S-32 [AB]); whether rabbits actively whisk in
  the rodent sense was not stated. **Mechanism:** cortical magnification of the muzzle.
  *Confidence: High* (map), Low (whisking).
- **Substrate-vibration sensing: no source found.** Whether rabbits perceive foot-thumps through the ground,
  through the air, or both is unstudied in what I could reach (see 13). *Unresolved.*
- **Pups use tactile and thermal cues to settle** (Pacheco-Cobos 2003, PubMed 12720035, [TI]).

### 13 · Communication channels

**Chemical (the best-documented channel).**
- **Chin (submandibular) gland and "chinning."** Both sexes chin-mark objects; males more, though females
  chin more than males around days 30–50; the gland is larger in males from puberty; castration shrinks the
  gland and reduces or abolishes chinning and reduces male aggression, and testosterone reverses this
  (S-28 [FT], citing González-Mariscal et al. 1992, 1993; Cerbón 1996 — secondary). Gland size and marking
  rise with social status and in the breeding season (S-28 citing Mykytowycz 1965; S-33 [TI]).
  **Mechanism:** androgen-dependent gland plus androgen-dependent motor pattern. *Confidence: Medium–High.*
- **Field marks are placed at the group's center and at boundaries; only dominant individuals' marks were
  identifiable; dominants also mark subordinates' heads** (S-36 [AB], `wild`, Australia). Chin-secretion
  chemistry differs between sites >100 km apart (S-37 [AB]). **When a male becomes dominant, a new compound,
  2-phenoxyethanol, appears in his secretion; rabbits cannot smell it, but it slows the release of the
  other volatiles** (a fixative), so dominant marks persist longer (S-38 [AB]). **Mechanism:** a
  status-linked change in gland chemistry. This is an involuntary status signal. *Confidence: Medium*
  (single lab line).
- **Chinning is triggered by novelty and by visual/tactile features of objects, not their odor** (domestic
  males: habituates within a session; re-stimulated only by visually different preferred objects)
  (S-39 [AB]); novel objects are chinned more after 5 min but not after longer delays (S-40). *Confidence:
  Medium (domestic).*
- **Inguinal-gland odor carries individual/group identity.** In outdoor pens of wild-type rabbits, group
  members smeared with inguinal secretion from unfamiliar same-sex rabbits were attacked by penmates; chin
  secretion, urine, anal-gland material, and perfume caused little or no disruption. In mixed-sex groups 60% of
  subordinate females smeared with unfamiliar males' inguinal secretion were attacked, mainly by the dominant
  males (S-34 [AB], n = 60 ♂, 120 ♀, 162 mixed). **Mechanism:** identity encoded in inguinal secretion (sex-
  and individual-specific chemistry also in S-33/Goodrich & Mykytowycz 1972 [TI]). *Confidence: High.*
- **Anal gland coats fecal pellets; pellet odor raises the owner's "territorial confidence."** Volatiles from a
  male's own pellets shifted paired territorial contests in his favor (64 adult males, 140 tests); both basic
  and acidic compounds were needed (S-35 [AB]). *Confidence: Medium–High.*
- **Latrines (dung hills) are communication sites, and their layout shifts with conditions.** Wild rabbits
  deposit pellets (coated with anal secretion) at latrines and chin-mark pellets. Rural populations near
  Frankfurt had the largest and densest latrines near the burrow (core marking, within-group); urban and
  suburban populations had larger latrines farther from the burrow (peripheral marking, between-group). The
  authors link this to higher urban densities but smaller groups (S-42 [FT]; S-41 [TI]). Residents
  counter-marked strangers' pellets more than neighbors' (a "dear enemy" pattern) but escalated against
  neighbors after repeated intrusions (S-43 [AB], native range, suburban Spain). **Variation recorded:**
  core-dominant (rural DE) vs peripheral-dominant (urban DE); Madrid suburban latrines decreased with
  distance from burrows (S-42 citing Domínguez-Cebrián & de Miguel 2013, secondary). *Confidence: High*
  (latrines as signal sites), Medium (drivers).
- **Urine.** Squat urination, urine squirts, and urine spraying ("enurination") aimed at another rabbit during
  courtship or aggression (S-45 [FT], `lab`). Whether urine carries a primer effect (e.g., a pregnancy block)
  is unstudied in rabbits (S-28). *Confidence: Medium (lab ethogram).*
- **Mother–young chemical channel:** see 5 (MP). Kits are reported to accept their mother's anal-gland odor
  more readily, and dominant males to mark group members, maintaining a "group scent" (S-28 citing
  Mykytowycz & Goodrich 1974 — **secondary, unverified**).
- **"Alarm pheromone": UNVERIFIED.** S-28 asserts rabbits release volatile alarm compounds (perianal) that
  cause fleeing, but its citations are a general/rat literature. Not admitted as a finding.

**Acoustic (thinly documented).**
- **Repertoire.** Low-pitched grunting (pursued, sexually aroused, or unreceptive females); very high-pitched
  screaming when injured or frightened; male vocalization at ejaculation (S-45 [FT], `lab`). Adults rarely if
  ever vocalize to minor procedures (S-51 [FT], citing earlier work). Pregnant domestic females placed with a
  male vocalized (75.3% of pregnant vs 7.6% of non-pregnant on day 12) (S-50 [FT]). **No systematic acoustic
  analysis of the adult repertoire was found** (dead end at sources).
- **Pup distress calls exist and work.** Pups only a few days old emit trains of high-pitched distress calls;
  in field playbacks next to nursery burrows, 60.6% of mothers rapidly returned, 40% of those inspected the
  entrance, equally at both test ages (S-49 [AB], `wild-encl`; S-85 [FT]). Pups reportedly vocalize more before
  nursing (Schuh et al. 2004, cited in S-50 — secondary). **Mechanism (proposed by authors):** a maternal
  response that may repel infanticidal females. *Confidence: High* (calls and response).
- **Ultrasonic calls: no evidence found** (dead end; see search log). The documented calls are audible to
  observers.

**Percussive / substrate.**
- **Thumping:** loud striking of the ground with the hind foot or feet, usually when alarmed; males may also
  thump after mating (S-45 [FT]). Septal lesions increase thumping along with escape and vocalization
  (S-58 [AB]). **Mechanism:** part of the species-typical fear repertoire, held in check by septal
  inhibition. **Receiver response untested** (S-47, S-48 [TI]; dead end). *Confidence: Medium* (existence,
  context), *Unresolved* (function for receivers).

**Visual.**
- **Tail-flagging (white underside).** In 2,169 wild escape events, adults with young nearby were about three
  times as likely to flag before fleeing from a human (22% vs 7%), and none flagged at an empty trolley:
  support for an alarm signal to kin before escape; in flight, some support for a pursuit-deterrent ("quality")
  function and a possible confusion effect at high density; vigilance/perception advertisement and group
  cohesion rejected (S-46 [FT], `wild` UK). Tail is also erect in aggressive/courtship interactions;
  unreceptive females tail-flag at males (S-45). *Confidence: High* (single large study).
- **Postural and ear signals.** Crouching with ears flattened (submissive "freeze"); "submitting" (nose
  dropped below an approaching rabbit's nose); bowing; ears erect = alert; parallel running along a mutual
  territorial boundary with paw-scraping and scent-marking displays (S-45 [FT], `lab` ethogram). Ears pressed
  back tends to track corticosterone (S-53). *Confidence: Medium.*

**Tactile.** Allogrooming (head, especially ears; typically of the opposite sex in lab groups), nudging,
lying in contact, chin-marking a conspecific (S-45 [FT]; S-36).

**Signal control (voluntary vs involuntary).**
- *Context-sensitive (audience-dependent):* pre-flight tail-flagging rises with kin present (S-46); pain
  facial expression differs with a human observer present (S-52, see 14); chinning habituates and sensitizes
  to novelty (S-39, S-40).
- *Physiologically fixed:* status-linked gland chemistry (S-38), androgen-dependent marking (S-28), identity
  odors (S-34). These cannot be "turned off" by the sender on the evidence available. *Confidence: Medium.*

### 14 · Emotion expression

- **Fear runs through bradycardia, via the central amygdala.** In aversive Pavlovian conditioning, rabbits
  develop a conditioned *slowing* of the heart; electrical stimulation of the amygdala central nucleus
  evokes bradycardia within ~1 s, and cell-body lesions there block acquisition of the conditioned
  bradycardia without affecting unlearned heart-rate responses (S-57 [AB], `lab`). Conditioned bradycardia
  first appears around PND 18 (S-16). **Mechanism:** CeA → medullary cardioregulatory nuclei.
  *Confidence: High.*
- **Tonic immobility (TI).** Inducible in rabbits; during TI heart rate falls, rhythm changes, cardiac output
  falls (S-54 [AB]). Hippocampal activity during TI resembles that during exposure to a stuffed sparrowhawk and
  during submissive posture with a conspecific; TI lasts longer at night (S-56 [AB]). "Trancing" pet rabbits
  was reported to be a fear state with *raised* heart and breathing rate and corticosterone (S-55 [SN]).
  **Variation recorded:** heart-rate direction differs between S-54 (decrease) and S-55 (increase,
  secondary); not averaged. Septal lesions enhance TI (S-58). *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Stress hormone: corticosterone.** Fecal corticosterone metabolites rise with the local index of mammalian
  carnivores across 20 Doñana populations (threat-sensitive; food and density mattered less) (S-60 [AB],
  `wild` native). Fox odor raised fecal metabolites in 11 naive rabbits, more in males (S-61 [SN]). Subordinate
  rabbits have higher corticosterone than dominants (S-83 [AB]; S-85). Ten minutes of stroking on an
  unfamiliar person's lap raised salivary corticosterone by +214% (n=7 domestic females); time spent in a
  tense posture correlated with the rise (r = 0.82) (S-53 [AB]). *Confidence: High.*
- **Pain face.** The Rabbit Grimace Scale uses orbital tightening, cheek flattening, nose shape (becoming
  pointed), whisker change, and ear position; at rest rabbits show a cheek and nose bulge that flattens with
  pain (S-51 [FT], `lab` NZW n=8). With a human observer present, scores were *lower* at peak pain and higher
  at baseline (n=28) (S-52 [AB]). S-51 offers prey-species masking as a *hypothesis* only. **Mechanism:**
  facial musculature; audience modulation of expression (mechanism unknown). *Confidence: Medium–High.*
- **Positive affect, measurable.** Food and nursing each produce conditioned place preference (nursing is
  rewarding for the lactating female) (S-75 [AB], `lab`). An anxiolytic dose of gabapentin increased
  frolicking and decreased "observing" (vigilance) without changing mobility (S-101 [AB], n=8). Read
  together: fear state and play trade off. *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Individual variation is real but not domain-general.** Low-scanning rabbits showed a large corticosterone
  rise to fox odor while high scanners stayed stable; early social behavior predicted later test responses,
  but responses were not consistent across tests (S-62 [AB], `wild-encl`). Pups already differ consistently
  (struggle latency, open-field activity), linked to their body mass relative to littermates (S-78 [AB]);
  huddle position and sibling relations shape these differences (S-104 [AB]). *Confidence: Medium.*
- **What domestication changed (mechanism of change; coordinate until a charter names domestic stock).**
  Domestic rabbits have smaller amygdala and larger medial prefrontal cortex volumes, and reduced white-matter
  anisotropy, versus wild rabbits (S-68 [AB]). Tameness is polygenic, with many sweeps near brain/neuronal
  development genes (S-69 [AB]). In introduced populations, domestication alleles are selected against, and the
  most parallel signals overlap neuronal genes (S-70 [AB]). *Confidence: High.*
- **A narrow early window sets fear of humans, but not fear of predators.** Daily handling in the first
  postpartum week, specifically between 15 min before and 30 min after the daily nursing, made wild-type and
  domestic rabbits approach humans at weaning and as adults; handling at other times did not; both handled and
  unhandled rabbits still avoided a stuffed fox (S-71, S-72 [AB]). The effective ingredient is the pups'
  pre-nursing arousal, not feeding (S-72, 2003). **Mechanism:** arousal-gated developmental learning that
  re-categorizes one stimulus class; the predator category stays. *Confidence: High.*

### 15 · Cognition

- **Learning capacities shown.** One-trial, pheromone-gated odor learning in neonates (S-20, S-22); prenatal
  flavor learning (S-23); classical conditioning of heart rate and eyelid/nictitating-membrane responses (the
  rabbit is a standard lab model; S-57 and PubMed hits in the search log); conditioned place preference
  (S-75); clicker training of target-following, stepping on a scale, entering a box, and rearing onto a hand,
  retained after breaks and only partly generalized to a second familiar person (13 NZW females) (S-76 [AB]).
  *Confidence: High (lab).*
- **Diet preferences pass from mother to young by three redundant routes** — in utero, through nursing, and
  through maternal fecal pellets left in the nest — each as effective as the others (S-73 [AB]); pups learned
  to prefer a toxic plant (milkweed) from pellets, and even when the mother ate it a month *before* pregnancy
  (S-74 [AB]). **Mechanism:** chemosensory learning windows; vertical transmission of a learned preference.
  (Per M-2, the *capacity* is a mechanism; any specific diet tradition is learned and does not cross.)
  *Confidence: High (lab/captive).*
- **Object memory (as measured): short-term only.** In a chin-marking novelty paradigm, novel-object
  discrimination appeared after 5 min but not after longer delays, and was disrupted by NMDA antagonists
  (S-40 [AB], `domestic`). This measures habituation/sensitization of a marking response, not memory capacity
  in general. *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Social memory:** residents treat neighbors' and strangers' pellets differently (S-43); group membership
  is tracked by odor (S-34). *Confidence: Medium–High.*
- **Spatial memory/navigation: no direct study found** (dead end). Indirect only (home ranges, burrow
  returns, latrine networks).
- **Attention and vigilance: two forms.** In the Bayreuth enclosure, "overt" vigilance rose when buzzards were
  present (both sexes), while "subtle" vigilance rose with conspecifics nearby (males near other males;
  females near either sex, but only females in socially unstable situations) (S-59 [AB]). On Kerguelen,
  rabbits avoided closed patches at night (when cats hunt there) and raised vigilance if they used them;
  habitat use and vigilance complement each other (S-66 [AB], `feral`). Group-size effects on vigilance:
  S-67 [TI]. *Confidence: High.*
- **Predator recognition: partly innate-by-history, partly flexible.** Introduced Australian rabbits avoided
  odors of predators they co-evolved with but not novel predators (S-63, 2010); a later study found they *also*
  avoided non-co-evolved marsupial predators, with responses stronger at low perceived risk and fading over
  time; the authors suggest learning over ~150 years (S-64, 2015). **Variation recorded, not averaged.**
  *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Personality vs problem solving:** no link in a small rescue sample; exploration time repeatable, novel-
  object latency not (S-77, **preprint**, n=15). *Confidence: Low.*
- **Brain variation:** domestic brain architecture differs (S-68). Urban rabbits had heavier brains
  (absolute) despite smaller bodies than rural rabbits (S-102 [AB]); no behavioral link tested.
  *Confidence: Low (urban brain).*

### 16 · Social instinct

- **Unit: a territorial breeding group sharing underground refuge.** Discrete breeding groups are individuals
  sharing access to refuges (S-82 [AB]). Typical composition 1–5 adult males and 1–8 adult females (S-85 [FT],
  summarizing Parer 1977, Cowan 1987, von Holst et al. 2002); "two or three males and several females" in
  confined Australian populations (S-80 [AB]). *Confidence: High.*
- **Dominance: separate linear rank orders in each sex.** Established and maintained by intense fighting;
  strongest at the onset of breeding, later ritualized into displacements, especially among females
  (S-83 [AB]; S-85 [FT]). High rank gives priority of access: males to females, females to good nursery-burrow
  sites (S-85; S-82). High-ranking males and females (at least by their second breeding season) had much
  higher lifetime fitness through higher fecundity and a ~50% longer reproductive lifespan, and lower
  psychosocial stress (S-83). **Variation recorded:** in a 10-cohort analysis, females that were
  higher-ranking *at the onset of their first breeding season* had *lower* lifetime reproductive success
  (S-90 [AB]); not reconciled here (different timing variable). Female rank tracks age; groups whose female
  hierarchy is age-graded have less aggression and less infanticide (S-85; S-86). *Confidence: High.*
- **Territoriality.** Males defend female access both indirectly (territory) and directly (within group);
  females defend nest-site access within groups; inter-group interactions among females are rare (S-82 [AB]).
  Dominant males range more widely and eject foreign males (S-80). Enclosure group territories ~2,000–2,800 m²
  (S-85). Scent-marked at center and boundary (S-36; S-42). *Confidence: High.*
- **Density changes the system.** As confined populations grew: more groups, smaller territories and home
  ranges; female–female aggression within groups rose; male aggression was "always high" (S-80 [AB]).
  Confirmed in Bayreuth: per-female agonism ~0.8/h in 2-female groups vs ~2.6/h in 6-female groups; litters and
  offspring per female fell with female density, for dominants and subordinates alike (S-85 [FT]). Along the
  Frankfurt gradient, urban sites had higher densities but much smaller groups (mean group size 1.5–4 urban vs
  8.8–10 rural) and smaller, simpler burrows (S-42 [FT] table; S-44 [AB]). A sparse New Zealand population had
  no warrens at all, sheltered in scrub by day and bred in "stops" (S-97 [AB]). Translocated rabbits in
  Spain were mostly alone or in pairs where terrestrial predators had access, and in pairs or larger groups
  where they were fenced out (S-65 [AB]). **Variation is the finding:** group size and warren use are
  condition-dependent. *Confidence: High.*
- **Dispersal and philopatry: male-biased on average, but variable and density-dependent.**
  - Webb et al. (6 years): significantly more males than females moved to a new group before first breeding;
    female philopatry reflected in DNA-fingerprint relatedness (S-92 [AB]).
  - Surridge et al.: females more related than males within groups and population-wide; within-group female
    relatedness twice between-group (S-93 [AB]); but in another analysis females "did not necessarily group
    with kin", and group number rose with population size (S-94 [AB]).
  - Künkele & von Holst (2-ha enclosure): 93% of males and 64% of females dispersed; female dispersers usually
    went to neighboring territories, males farther; females that stayed produced more offspring (S-91 [SN]).
  - Richardson et al. (Australia, 4 habitats): philopatry of young **18–90% for males and 32–95% for females**
    across populations; male-biased in some but not all; after an artificial crash, both sexes moved farther
    at low density; at high density both sexes dispersed at a similar low rate (~20%); genetic structuring in
    adult females in 4 of 5 populations (S-95 [AB]).
  - Parer (Urana, 5 years): 307 dispersal movements, more by males; adults rarely changed warrens (S-96 [AB]).
  - Litter sisters sometimes dispersed together into a neighboring group (S-85).
  **Mechanism candidates:** density-dependent switching between dispersal and philopatry, with sex-specific
  thresholds (S-95); resident adults chasing juveniles out in late summer/autumn, more at high density
  (S-85 citing S-80, S-82). *Confidence: High (pattern and its variability).*
- **Affiliation.** Females with litter sisters in their group had more positive interactions (with the sisters
  and with other females) from juvenility onward and began breeding ~14 days earlier (S-88 [AB]). More time
  near mother and littermates after weaning predicted lower corticosterone and lower nematode loads
  ("social buffering") (S-85 [FT]; S-103). In the lab, females worked for limited social contact nearly as hard
  as for food (S-79 [SN]). Juveniles are highly interconnected within and between nearby warrens, unlike adults
  (S-98 [AB]). Lying in contact and allogrooming are part of the repertoire (S-45). *Confidence: High.*
- **Aggression toward young and newcomers.** Adult–juvenile aggression was mainly intra-sexual; females
  initiated more; males sometimes took a protective role; males interrupted female–female fights (S-82 [AB]).
  Newcomers to an experimental colony were attacked, by dominants especially (S-81 [SN]). *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Infanticide by non-mother adult females.** Infanticide affected ~5% of litters (12% of litter losses),
  only in the first 10 days, unrelated to density or the mother's rank, and ~7% more frequent where same-aged
  females held adjacent ranks (S-86 [AB]); 2–6% of litters per season, absent in some years; perpetrators
  scrape out the nest when taking over a nursery burrow (S-85 [FT]); 13 litters in an earlier 2.2-ha study
  (S-87 [AB]). **Mechanism:** competition for costly nursery burrows. *Confidence: High.*
- **Mother–young relationship (social side only).** Nursing is the only mother–pup contact: once a day, inside
  the nest, ~3 (3–5) minutes (S-99 [AB], `lab/domestic`). But in the field enclosure mothers are not
  "absent": they stay closer to the nursery burrow for the first ~10 days (17 m vs 23 m), chase other females
  away from its entrance, return to pup distress calls (S-49, S-85, S-86), and were seen defending emerged
  kits against a magpie (anecdotal, S-85). Kits emerge ~day 20 (S-85). Domestic females show "nonselective
  nursing" (S-99, 2022). *Confidence: High.*
- **Kin recognition: NOT demonstrated** in any source reached. What *is* shown: odor-based recognition of
  group membership/identity (S-34), neighbor–stranger discrimination (S-43), and sibling association
  (S-88). Whether recognition is by kinship or by familiarity is untested. *Unresolved.*
- **Revised-model watch.**
  - Myers & Poole 1959 framed a "strict dominance hierarchy among the males"; later work extends hierarchy to
    *both* sexes (S-83). Refinement, not reversal.
  - Myers & Poole 1961 attributed most home-range shifts to young adults being "forced" to move; later genetic
    and field work shows dispersal is *variable and density-dependent* (S-95), with high female dispersal in at
    least one enclosure (S-91). The simple "males leave, females stay" model is revised to "male-biased on
    average, condition-dependent" (feeds HAR-ULM-38 below).
  - Huang et al. 2025 state that rabbits "often rear young communally" (S-46). This conflicts with separate
    nursery burrows and female infanticide (S-85, S-86) and is not supported by any primary source here.
    Logged as a dead end; not used.

### Cross-domain · Predator vigilance and anti-predator behavior

- **Escape to refuge is the primary defense; cover is evaluated as "area to cover."** Flight-initiation distance
  was larger for a human, a fox model, and (marginally) a live buzzard than for a stuffed rabbit (S-46). On
  Kerguelen, fine-scale habitat use tracked the area of cover around a point better than distance to nearest
  cover (S-66).
- **Threat-sensitive physiology** (S-60) and **two vigilance modes** (S-59) (see 14, 15).
- **Predator odors:** see S-61, S-62, S-63, S-64, S-100 (review: prey inhibit activity, suppress
  non-defensive behavior, shift habitat; stress hormones rise).
- **Alarm behavior:** pre-flight tail-flag to kin (S-46); thumping (S-45); screams when caught (S-45).
- **Last-resort immobility:** TI (S-54, S-56).
- **Fear of humans is plastic, fear of predators is not (by early handling)** (S-71, S-72).
- **Urban relaxation:** urban rabbits are reported to show extended activity and reduced anti-predator
  behavior (S-42 citing Ziege et al., secondary; Ziege et al. 2016 *Mamm Biol*, [TI]). *Confidence: Low.*

### Cross-domain · Play

- **Form:** locomotor play — head flicking; "play gambolling"/"frisky hop" (forward hops with sideways
  tossing of head and ears, body twists, kicks); young rabbits run back and forth (S-45 [FT], `lab`).
- **State dependence:** an anxiolytic increased frolicking and reduced vigilance (S-101). Owner-reported jumping
  play (the source's term: "binky behaviour (joy jumps)") was less frequent in rabbits with ear problems
  (S-17, owner survey).
- **Social play-fighting in wild juveniles: not found** (dead end). Juveniles' dense contact networks (S-98)
  do not establish play. *Confidence: Medium (locomotor play in captivity); Unresolved (wild, social play).*

### Mechanism highlights for M-1 (input, not Step C)

These are the mechanism-level findings most relevant to D-7's "the animal side always remains in behavior."
They are candidates for M-1 to test, not resolutions.
1. **Horizon-weighted panoramic vision coupled to coarse hearing** (S-03, S-04, S-12, S-14): attention is
   built to scan a horizon band and swing gaze toward sounds, not to fixate a single point.
2. **Fear expressed as heart-slowing and freezing via the central amygdala**, with TI as a last resort
   (S-54, S-56, S-57). Fear is structurally different from a fight-or-flight-only model.
3. **An arousal-gated early window re-categorizes "humans" but not "predators"** (S-71, S-72): a precise
   developmental lever with a built-in limit.
4. **Chemical identity and status signals that the sender does not control** (S-34, S-38): group membership
   read from inguinal odor; dominance written into chin-gland chemistry.
5. **Pheromone-gated one-trial learning and vertical diet-flavor transmission** (S-20, S-22, S-73, S-74).
6. **Sex-separate rank orders, density-dependent aggression and dispersal, nursery-site competition**
   (S-83, S-85, S-86, S-95): social structure is a set of condition-dependent rules, not a fixed form.
7. **Audience-modulated expression** (tail-flag with kin present, pain face with an observer present)
   (S-46, S-52).
8. **Domestication acts on fear circuitry** (amygdala/mPFC, polygenic, reversed in feral populations)
   (S-68–S-70): the size of the fear system is a variable a creator could set, which bears on D-7 variance.

   native range was found (searches #92–#94). Nearly all social-structure data are from introduced or
   enclosure populations of *O. c. cuniculus* stock.

   Hughes 1971 (S-05) and Heesy 2004 likely hold them; both behind access barriers.

5. **Vocal repertoire.** No acoustic analysis of adult calls; pup-call parameters are in S-49's full text
   (not reached). Ultrasonic channel: no evidence either way beyond absence in the literature reached.

   1969 and Randall 2001 not read beyond metadata.
7. **Adult olfactory acuity and VNO function in adult social behavior.**
8. **Kin recognition** (vs familiarity) — untested.
9. **Spatial memory and navigation** — no study found.
10. **Play in wild juveniles** and social play-fighting.
11. **Lateralization / paw preference.**
12. **Alarm pheromone** — claimed in a review (S-28) without rabbit-specific primary support.

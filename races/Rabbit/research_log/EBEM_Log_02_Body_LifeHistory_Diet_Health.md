# EBEM Research Log 02 — Body, Life History, Diet, Health (O. cuniculus)
**Status:** M-2 Steps A–B, 2026-10-21. Step C (retention against L1) pending M-1.

**Scope of this log:** L1 domains 1 (body plan, scale, locomotion), 2 (manipulation), 7 (diet, dentition,
digestion), 8 (activity cycle and sleep), 9 (thermoregulation and environmental tolerance), 10 (reproduction),
11 (development and maturation), 12 (lifespan and aging), and 17 (health and vulnerabilities), plus population
dynamics and rabbit-specific body-size data for the allometry work. Senses, communication, cognition, and social
structure belong to Log 01 (`EBEM_Log_01_Senses_Communication_Cognition_Social.md`) and appear here only as
context. Everything below is `Proposed:` input data (CLAUDE.md rule 6). No race trait is written here.

**Step 0 compliance notes.**
- **LAW 0-R.** Every fact below was retrieved during this session. Nothing is filled from memory. Where a number
  reached me only through a search-engine summary or a citing paper, it is tagged `[SN]` and treated as
  provisional. Dead ends, and where each died, are in the Search log.
- **Coordinate, never a cause.** Study sites, breeds, lab strains, farm systems, and introduction histories are
  recorded only as coordinates (Step F field). Domestication findings are logged as *mechanisms of change*
  (genes, hormones), not as reasons for any race trait. Per M-2's sequencing rule they become causal only if a
  lineage charter names domestic stock.
- **Folk meaning.** None used. Pet-care and breeder pages were read only as leads to primary work and are tagged
  `[LEAD]`; no claim rests on them alone.
- **Role nouns.** None imported. "Dominant," "subordinate," and "rank" are the sources' operational terms for
  agonistic outcomes.
- **Variation is data.** Where populations or studies disagree, both values are recorded side by side. Nothing is
  averaged.

**Access legend** (same as Log 01, plus one tag). This sets the ceiling on confidence:
`[FT]` full text read (PDF converted locally) · `[FT*]` full text read through a web-fetch extraction (numbers
as reported by the extractor; re-check before final use) · `[AB]` abstract read directly (PubMed / Crossref /
publisher) · `[SN]` findings seen only via a search-engine summary or a citing paper · `[TI]` title/metadata only
· `[LEAD]` non-peer-reviewed lead.

**Management-status tags:** `wild` (free-living, native or introduced) · `wild-encl` (wild-origin animals in a
large semi-natural field enclosure, with natural predation) · `wild-pen` (wild-type animals in small outdoor
pens or cages) · `feral` · `domestic` (pet, farm) · `lab` (laboratory strain, usually New Zealand White or Dutch)
· `captive` (other captivity).

**Step A hypothesis check (RWBEM: "lower tiers are often the most valuable").** It holds, but differently by
domain:
- **Physiology** (digestion, ovulation, nest-building hormones, nursing mechanism, ear thermoregulation, sleep)
  comes almost entirely from the Supporting tier (lab) and the domestic Secondary tier (farm, pet). No wild
  sleep, ovulation-mechanism, or cecotrophy-timing data exist in what I could reach.
- **Life history and population dynamics** come mainly from the Secondary tier: introduced Australian and New
  Zealand populations and a wild-origin German field-enclosure colony (Bayreuth).
- **The Primary tier (native Iberian range)** gave the reproduction season and litter size of *O. c. algirus*
  (Portugal), the predator-regime plasticity of the activity cycle (Spain), wild nutritional ecology (Spain), and
  the disease-driven decline series (Doñana, central Spain). It gave no physiology.
- **Consequence for M-1:** almost every *mechanism* below was measured in lab or farm animals, while almost every
  *wild expression* (timing, rates, mortality) was measured in introduced or enclosure populations. The two
  layers must be joined at M-1 with care, and the join is flagged wherever it is made.

## Inventory by L1 domain

Each bullet gives the finding, its S-codes and population status, the **mechanism** where one was found, the
**variation** between populations or studies, and a confidence rating:
**High** (several sources or full text, consistent) · **Medium** (one good source, or abstracts only) ·
**Low** (secondary or `[SN]` only, or sources conflict). "Derived" marks arithmetic or inference I made from
sourced numbers; it is not a source claim. "#n" points to row n of the Search log.

### 1 · Body plan, scale, and locomotion

**Body mass and size**
- **Wild adults are small: about 1.0–1.5 kg** by one genetics paper's statement (S-122, [FT*]). A secondary
  account gives 1.5–2.5 kg and 38–50 cm (Animal Diversity Web, `[LEAD]`, Search log #59). In four Australian
  introduced populations, kittens under ~600 g had not left their birth warren and animals over 1,250 g had
  dispersed and joined adult groups (S-07, wild). *Confidence: Medium* (no wild table of adult masses by sex was
  reachable; see gaps).
- **Subspecies differ.** *O. c. algirus* (south-west Iberia) is lighter, with shorter ears and hind feet, than
  *O. c. cuniculus*, across 999 adults from 27 native-range locations. Sex and geography also explain part of the
  variation (S-123, wild, [AB]; means not in the abstract). A Portuguese growth study says *algirus* grows less
  and for a shorter time (#117, [SN]). Plasma metabolomes of the two subspecies also differ (S-134,
  [AB]). *Confidence: Medium.*
- **Introduced populations vary.** Rabbits in Neuquén, Argentina, were heavier than European, Australian, and
  Chilean rabbits; the authors suggest low predation or genetic expression (S-127, [SN]). *Low.*
- **Sexual size dimorphism: direction unresolved.** S-123 reports a sex effect without (in the abstract) a
  direction. Neuquén females were heavier and larger than males (S-127, [SN]). A wildlife website claims males are
  more heavily built, with no data (#118, `[LEAD]`). A secondary summary claims "reverse" (female-biased)
  dimorphism is stronger in *algirus* (#117, [SN]). *Low; record both; do not average.*
- **Domestic range is very wide (coordinate only).** Most domestic breeds exceed 3 kg; dwarf breeds weigh under
  1 kg (S-122). UK pet rabbits: median adult body weight 2.1 kg (IQR 1.7–2.6; S-58); population mean about
  2.3 kg; growth reaches a stable adult weight at about 13 months (S-59). Breed classes run from <1 kg to >5 kg
  (S-132). *Mechanism:* dwarfism is largely a 12.1 kb deletion at *HMGA2* on chromosome 4. It is recessive lethal
  (homozygotes die within days of birth); heterozygotes reach about two-thirds of normal size (S-122). Small size
  in dwarf breeds is polygenic with a few major loci (S-132). Domestication overall shifted allele frequencies at
  many loci, with regulatory changes enriched near brain and neuronal-development genes (S-121, [SN]). *High*
  (for domestic data); causal for a race only if a charter names domestic stock.
- **Skeleton is light.** About 7–8% of body mass versus 12–13% in the cat (veterinary textbook) and "about 8%"
  in a rabbit anatomy paper (S-126, `[LEAD]`). Hind limbs exceed 42% of carcass mass in farm rabbits (#68,
  `[LEAD]`). A primary measurement was not found. *Low.*

**Body shape and skeleton, by size**
- **Across 40 lagomorph species, body size best predicts body shape and most limb traits.** Larger lagomorphs
  have stouter bodies with longer, more gracile forelimbs; smaller ones have more elongate bodies with shorter,
  more robust forelimbs. Burrowing habit and locomotor mode were poor predictors (S-117, [AB]). *Medium.* This is
  an allometric datum to pass to the general allometry agent.
- **Leporid limb proportions.** The leporid body type is defined by elongated hind legs, especially the shank and
  foot, and has been conservative since the Oligocene (S-125, [SN]). In CT data on ~40 Australian *O. cuniculus*
  and ~40 *L. europaeus*, relative limb length and joint velocity were lower than predicted by the old
  "cursoriality gradient." The authors conclude that body size and burrowing also shape the lagomorph skeleton
  (S-116, [AB]). *Medium.*
- **Cranial kinesis.** Leporids have an intracranial joint, the only cranial kinesis in mammals, hypothesized to
  dissipate kinetic energy during high-speed locomotion. Its suture complexity correlates with locomotor mode
  (less complex in more cursorial species), not with size or burrowing (S-118, [AB]). *Medium; function is a
  hypothesis.*

**Gait**
- **Bounding/half-bounding with a digitigrade gait on a plantigrade foot.** Rabbits are described as "among the
  few mammals that almost exclusively" bound or half-bound (Dagg 1973, cited in S-115). In six New Zealand White
  (NZW) rabbits, plantar contact covered 70 ± 11% of the plantar surface, shifted toward the toes, so the gait is
  digitigrade even though foot morphology is plantigrade. Peak vertical ground reaction force was 44 ± 11% of body
  weight; stance lasted about 0.40 s (S-115, lab, [FT*]). Rabbits "more frequently saltate," while hares are more
  cursorial (S-116). *Medium* (lab animals, stance phase only; no full-stride kinematics found).
- A claimed comparison of rabbit (forelimb-initiated) vs jackrabbit (hindlimb-initiated) half-bounds could not be
  traced to its source (Search log #67). *Unverified.*

**Burrowing and warrens**
- **Warrens range from a few to more than 100 entrances** (S-120 citing Kolb 1985 and Parer et al. 1987). In
  Doñana, pastureland warrens were about twice the size of scrubland warrens; 79.7–87.9% of scrubland warren
  surface lay under tall shrubs, and warrens were built among tree roots more in pasture. The author reads this
  as selecting supports against collapse (S-120, wild, [FT]). In closed scrub habitats, rabbits may live
  above ground and use seasonally isolated breeding burrows (S-120 citing Gibb & Fitzgerald 1998). *High.*
- **Breeding stops.** Females dig short breeding burrows ("stops", 0.5–2.0 m) and seal the entrance (S-02, wild-
  encl). Mothers close and disguise the burrow after each nursing visit (S-31). *High.*
- **Soil matters.** Rabbit populations in central-southern Spain held steady or grew mostly on soft soils, "where
  warren building is easier" (S-71, wild). *Medium.*
- **Burrows carry risk.** Young are killed by flooding, blocking, or collapse of warrens in heavy rain,
  especially in open habitat (S-120). *Medium.*
- **Domestic stock still digs.** Group-housed domestic does given soil dug burrows; one genotype (Mecklenburg) on
  soil showed the least aggression, fewest injuries, and lowest kit mortality (S-124, domestic). *Medium.*

### 2 · Manipulation

- **The forearm is locked in pronation.** In 20 domestic rabbit forearms, the radius and ulna are strongly curved
  and joined along their whole length by a short fibrous interosseous ligament; the olecranon's articular head is
  flattened, as in horse, ruminant, and pig. The authors class the rabbit forelimb with species whose forearm
  bones are "fixed in pronation position," so the forelimb's function is support and locomotion; no supination
  is possible (S-119, [FT]). By contrast the cat can supinate up to about 80° (same source). *Medium (one
  osteological source). Mechanism: anatomical (bone fusion by ligament, joint-surface shape).*
- **What the forepaws are documented doing:** digging warrens and breeding stops (S-02, S-120), and climbing into
  trees and shrubs to reach succulent leaves and twigs in drought (S-97, wild, NE South Australia). Diet includes
  corms of Guildford grass (*Romulea rosea*) in south-western Australia (S-96); reaching corms implies digging for
  food (*Derived*, not stated by S-96).
- **Nest-building movements:** "straw-carrying" and "hair-pulling" from the doe's own chest and flanks (S-22,
  S-31). The sources I reached do not say which body part performs each action. *Unverified mechanics.*
- **What I could not find:** any study of rabbit forepaw grasping, food handling, grooming kinematics, or paw
  preference. Every search returned rodent dexterity work only (Search log #75, #78, #80). This is a real gap,
  not an absence of the trait. *No confidence assigned.*

### 7 · Diet, dentition, and digestion

**Diet in the wild**
- **Generalist grazer that selects.** Stomach-content and fecal studies show grasses as the base (Neuquén,
  Argentina; Holkham dunes, England, where *Festuca rubra* dominated), with seasonal selection of particular
  grasses before flowering and heavy seed intake afterward (Wales) (#34, #107, [SN]). In south-western Australia,
  the diet was flexible, switched between monocots and dicots by habitat and season, relied strongly on seeds in
  late spring and summer, moved from pasture (winter) to scrub dicots (summer), and included high-water plants
  (>54% water) in summer (S-96, [AB]). In New Zealand's Mackenzie Basin, rabbits ate a mean of 11 plant species
  per stomach; six species made up 11% of the available vegetation but 44% of the diet (#34, [SN]). *Medium.*
- **Wild Mediterranean gastric contents (native range):** relative composition 8.8% ash, 25.5% crude protein,
  40.4% neutral detergent fiber, 25.4% highly digestible non-nitrogen nutrients (n = 80; S-92). Young rabbits'
  stomach contents had a higher protein share; adults ate more in absolute terms; adult females carried 85% more
  gastric content than males (S-93, n = 70). Pregnant does were 12% heavier and had 46% lower free fatty acids than
  lactating does (S-94, n = 29). *Medium* (abstracts). *Interpretation flag:* farm data show cecotrophs can make up
  about three quarters of stomach contents by late morning (S-01), so wild gastric protein may partly reflect
  cecotrophs. This is my inference; the wild studies did not test it.
- **Diet is learned perinatally, by three routes.** Pups prefer at weaning the foods their mother ate, learned
  equally well (1) in utero, (2) through nursing contact, and (3) from fecal pellets the mother leaves in the nest
  (S-51, lab). Pups given pellets from milkweed-fed rabbits on days 1–10 preferred milkweed at weaning (day 28),
  and even a maternal diet one month *before* pregnancy carried over (S-52, domestic). *Mechanism:* perinatal
  chemosensory learning; it is a mechanism that can cross the engineering boundary (M-2), even though any specific
  food tradition cannot. *Medium–High.*

**Dentition**
- **All teeth grow continuously and never form roots** (aradicular hypsodont, "elodont"). Veterinary sources give
  upper incisors ~2.0 mm/week, lower ~2.4 mm/week, cheek teeth ~2.5–3 mm/month (#32, `[LEAD]`; primary
  measurements not found). *Low for the numbers; High for continuous growth.*
- **Eruption tracks wear.** In a 17-month trial with 32 Dutch rabbits on four diets, incisor eruption matched
  attrition in every group, and both were *higher* on hay only. Muesli diets produced longer, more curved cheek
  teeth, wider interdental spaces, and dental disease in three muesli-only rabbits (S-63, domestic). *Mechanism:*
  tooth length is held steady by matching eruption to abrasive wear; soft food breaks the match. *Medium–High.*

**Digestive mechanism (all lab or farm data)**
- **Hindgut fermenter with a colonic separation mechanism (CSM).** The proximal colon sorts digesta: haustral
  contractions send fine particles back to the cecum, and segmental contractions push particles larger than about
  0.5 mm toward the anus as hard pellets (S-84). The *fusus coli*, a muscular, mucus-gland-rich segment, acts as
  the colonic pacemaker that switches between hard- and soft-feces production and wraps soft pellets in mucus
  (S-84, S-86). Lagomorphs use a "wash-back" CSM, rodents a "mucus-trap" CSM; in a rabbit–guinea pig comparison the
  rabbit's was the more efficient at extracting bacteria (S-90, [SN]). Surgical removal of the proximal colon abolished soft feces and
  cecotrophy (1979 study, #29, [SN]). *High.*
- **Cecotrophy (eating soft, cecum-derived pellets).** Soft pellets are about 27% dry matter; of that, ~30%
  protein, ~22% crude fiber, ~11% minerals, and about half the pellet is bacteria (S-01). They carry bacteria,
  volatile fatty acids (VFAs), vitamins, amino acids, and enzymes, and are protected from stomach acid by a mucus
  coat (S-84, S-86). Cecal VFAs supply about 40% of maintenance energy (S-84 citing Marty & Vernay 1984).
  Cecotrophy is the route by which B vitamins from gut bacteria are used (S-01). Preventing it (collars) slows
  growth in young rabbits and slowly lowers intake and weight in adults (#37, [SN]). *High.*
- **Timing (domestic):** soft pellets are produced about 4 h after feeding during quiet periods (S-84), or 8–12 h
  after feeding in rationed animals, mostly in the morning (S-01); once or twice per 24 h (S-84, S-86). *Wild
  timing and location (e.g., in the burrow) are unknown* (#33, dead end). *Medium.*
- **Transit and capacity (farm):** stomach 3–6 h, small intestine ~1.5 h, cecum 2–12 h, whole tract 18–30 h
  (mean ~20 h); cecum 40–45 cm long holding 100–120 g of paste at ~20% dry matter; stomach holds 60–80 g (S-01).
  Particle retention is short: the lowest of rabbits, equines, and ruminants on the same hay (solid mean retention
  ~5.3 h; S-91, [SN]), with fluid retained longer than particles (S-90, [SN]). *Medium.*
- **Feeding pattern (domestic):** 34–40 small meals per day; feeding time over 3 h/day at 6 weeks, under 2 h later;
  "much more is consumed in the dark" (S-01). Heat cuts intake (182 → 123 g/day from 5 to 30 °C) and meal number
  (37 → 27) (S-01). *Medium.*
- **Fiber is required, not optional.** Indigestible crude fiber below about 10% of the ration brings a risk of
  "lethal diarrhoea" (S-01). Low fiber shifts cecal VFAs from acetate toward propionate and butyrate (S-84).
  *Medium–High.*
- **Stomach and gut development.** Suckling pups' stomach pH is ~5–6.5; adults' 1–2 (S-84). Pups eat the 3–4
  fecal pellets the doe drops in the nest at each nursing; *Bacteroides* colonize the cecum from day 3, and pups
  denied pellets had 14–40% lower counts (S-50). Cecotrophy starts at about 3 weeks and is established by 6 weeks
  (S-01, S-84). *Medium–High.*
- **Body size and the cecotrophy strategy (coherence-critical).** A meta-analysis argues that a CSM coupled with
  coprophagy is *necessary below a body-size threshold* where living off body reserves or migrating is not
  feasible, to limit fecal nutrient losses (S-88). Leporids are the largest reingesting species apart from the
  coypu (S-87, [SN]). Small herbivores face a high metabolic-requirement-to-gut-capacity ratio, which forces rapid
  passage, hindgut fermentation, and selective feeding (S-89, [SN]); later work qualifies the claim that larger
  size automatically improves digestion (#39, [SN]). *Medium. For M-1:* the published rationale for cecotrophy is
  tied to small size. A human-sized body is far outside the size range of any known cecotroph.
- **Water.** Captive wild-caught rabbits could not reduce their water requirement below about 55% of total food
  plus water intake; dehydrated rabbits produced urine up to 1.9 M urea (S-98). Free-living arid-zone rabbits'
  water turnover tracked pasture hydration (r = 0.987), from 214 ml/kg/day (cool, wet) to 55 ml/kg/day (moderate
  drought); young rabbits turned water over faster than adults (S-99). In north-eastern South Australia, a
  population declined sharply when overgrazing removed succulent plants; rabbits with access to water kept their weight,
  and others climbed shrubs for moist leaves and ate woody twigs too poor in energy (S-97). Lactating farm does
  drink 200–250 g per kg body weight per day (S-01). *High.*

**Vomiting**
- **"Rabbits cannot vomit" is widely stated but not proven.** A 2025 critical review finds the direct evidence
  thin; retching was reported in rabbits given protoveratrine-A (Tanaka & Kanno 1956) and anecdotally with
  apomorphine; lagomorphs and rodents share a less muscular diaphragm with a larger central tendon (S-77). A
  veterinary source attributes it to the cardia–stomach arrangement (#102, `[LEAD]`). *Revised-model watch: treat
  as "not demonstrated," not "impossible."*

**Toxicities and detoxification**
- **Avocado (persin):** 14 domestic rabbits died of congestive heart failure within 30 h of eating fresh avocado
  leaves (S-79); rabbits are listed as sensitive to persin cardiotoxicosis, and *Allium* causes hemolytic anemia
  (S-80). *Medium.*
- **Atropine tolerance varies within the species.** Some rabbits carry serum atropinesterase, which hydrolyzes
  atropine to tropic acid and tropine; "some but not all" rabbits' plasma breaks down large amounts. Inheritance
  is reported as recessive (1943) or as autosomal incomplete dominance (OMIA, citing 1958); no causal variant is
  known (S-78, [SN]). *Low–Medium. Mechanism: enzymatic, genetically variable.*
- Pet-care lists (yew, foxglove, lily of the valley, ivy, rhododendron, nightshade, ragwort) had no primary
  rabbit data behind them (#105, `[LEAD]`). No peer-reviewed rabbit plant-toxicity review was found.

### 8 · Activity cycle and sleep

- **The circadian clock is nocturnal-type.** Rabbits' phase response to light pulses matches other
  predominantly nocturnal mammals, and they entrain by discrete phase shifts (S-33, lab, n = 12). *Medium–High.*
- **Expressed activity is plastic and set by conditions** (record all; do not average):
  - Dutch coastal dunes: one nocturnal above-ground period with irregular fluctuations; much less time above
    ground November–February; emergence and retreat times stayed about the same all year instead of tracking
    sunset and sunrise; wild rabbits more nocturnal than domestic ones (S-101, wild, [SN]).
  - Australian enclosure under artificial light, late spring: feeding throughout the night, peak at 21:00 (S-104).
  - German enclosure: peak adult activity in the last 4–5 h before dawn (S-02 citing Wallage-Drees 1989).
  - Spanish semi-free enclosures: "markedly crepuscular and nocturnal" with two peaks at sunrise and sunset;
    males more active than females; more active in the first half of the year; less active at temperature
    extremes, high humidity, and strong wind; on brighter moonlit nights, more activity but shorter stays in
    feeding areas (S-102).
  - Central Spain translocation: nocturnal in the donor area (red fox abundant); **crepuscular, with two peaks
    around sunrise and sunset**, in an unfenced release area with five carnivore species; nocturnal in a fenced
    plot where the strictly diurnal Egyptian mongoose dominated (S-103).
  - *Mechanism of plasticity:* predator regime (S-103), weather (S-102), and season (S-101). *High that the
    pattern varies; Medium for each value.*
- **Feeding and nursing sit inside the dark phase.** Most domestic feeding happens in the dark (S-01); 84–86% of
  nursing visits happen at night (S-32); lab nursing clusters around 03:51 h (S-29).
- **Winter energy saving.** In a semi-natural enclosure, overwinter food shortage lowered heart rate by about 30%,
  with lower daily metabolic rate, body weight, and blood sugar, while telemetered body temperature stayed
  remarkably stable (S-100). *Medium. Mechanism: metabolic depression without hypothermia.*
- **Sleep (lab only).** Adult male rabbits slept 11.4 h per day: 25.9% drowsy, 64.5% slow-wave sleep, 9.6%
  paradoxical (REM) sleep, with more wakefulness and less REM in the dark phase (S-105). Another lab study found a
  "diurnal preference for sleep" (S-106). NZW rabbits can sleep with their eyes open (S-107, n = 3). Sleep bout
  structure (polyphasic or not) was not in the abstracts I read. **No wild or semi-natural sleep data exist in what
  I reached.** *Medium (lab); wild unknown.*
- **Coherence link to vision (Log 01's domain):** the NZW retina has rods peaking near 300,000/mm² and cones near
  18,000/mm² (S-108), a rod-rich design consistent with dim-light activity. Pass to M-1 as a coherence check only.

### 9 · Thermoregulation and environmental tolerance

- **Ears are thermoregulatory radiators under central neural control.** Ear blood flow and vascular conductance
  rise with core temperature during whole-body heating until a plateau (S-110). Active ear vasodilation needs
  nitric oxide (S-111); a central NO donor triggers coordinated heat defense: ear skin warming, faster panting, and
  more respiratory evaporative water loss (S-112). Cold-induced sympathetic vasoconstriction of the ear arteries
  depends on neurons in the rostral medullary raphé (S-109). The ear's arteriovenous anastomoses (AVAs) are under
  alpha-adrenergic and NO control (#85, lab). *High (lab mechanism).*
- **Heat is the weak side.** Farm sources put comfort or thermoneutrality around 18–21 °C (other sources 15–25 or
  16–22 °C), heat stress above ~30 °C, and loss of temperature control above ~35 °C; respiration rose from 69 to
  190 breaths/min between 18.3 and 35.3 °C in an older study; sweat glands are described as non-functional
  (#82, #83, S-114 via citing pages, [SN]). Above 30 °C buck libido falls, 8 h at 36 °C reduces sperm motility, and
  embryo mortality rises above 30–33 °C (S-01). Heat stress harms immune, endocrine, antioxidant, and
  reproductive function (S-81). *Medium (farm data; wild limits not measured).*
- **Arid survival depends on water from plants and on burrows.** See water findings in domain 7 (S-97–S-99).
  Rabbits living with hot summer soil need free water, succulent plants, shaded warrens, or warrens in calcareous
  soil (Parer & Libke 1985, cited in #87, [SN]). Burrow air in arid South Australia stayed above 70% (usually
  80–90%) relative humidity for at least 4–5 months of winter and spring where fleas occurred (S-76). **No study
  reached gives the temperature-buffering amplitude of a warren.** *Medium.*
- **Cold and winter.** In the German enclosure, subadult overwinter survival depended most on autumn body mass and
  winter temperature, then density and sex (females survived better); autumn mass depended on birth date, density,
  and the share of rainy days in the first two months after emergence (S-44). In Dutch dunes, adults carried more
  fat and died less in every winter; starvation occurred only in a very severe winter and hit juveniles most (S-131,
  [SN]). On Lemnos, fat builds in summer and falls from late summer into autumn (S-129, [SN]). Wild rabbits are
  lean, and protein may make up to 40% of their energy reserves (S-130, [SN]). *Medium.*
- **Neonates cannot thermoregulate alone.** Pups are born near-poikilothermic: furless, with a high
  surface-to-volume ratio and immature vasomotor control. They become homeothermic over the first ~15 days,
  sooner in heavier pups that hold central huddle positions and grow fur faster (S-48). The mother does not brood
  them (S-45). Peripheral pups express more uncoupling protein 1 (brown fat thermogenesis) than central pups
  (S-45). Rabbit pups, unlike several rat strains, show no torpor-like circadian core-temperature rhythm (S-113).
  Nest temperature tracks soil temperature (3–21 °C across a breeding season) and litter size (S-38). *High.*

### 10 · Reproduction

**Ovulation and the absence of a cycle**
- **Induced (reflex) ovulator with no regular estrous cycle.** Ovulation follows mating; it is not a cyclic event
  (S-13, S-10, S-01). Does are receptive much of the time but variably: does with a red vulva accept mating and
  ovulate about 90% of the time versus about 10% otherwise (S-01). Ovulation occurs about 10–12 h after mating
  (S-01; 10–13 h in a secondary review, #93); in 30 Hycole does, ovulatory follicles were present 10 h after
  natural mating or insemination but not after saline (S-12). *High.*
- **Mechanism (disputed).** The core pathway is coitus → hypothalamic GnRH → pituitary LH surge → ovulation
  (S-10, S-13). What starts it is disputed:
  - *Neural/genital:* In S-09, ovulation followed natural mating in 83.3% of does and raw-semen insemination in
    66.7%, but only 16.7% when the lumbar spinal pathway was anesthetized, and 0% with saline. A neural route
    "facilitates" ovulation.
  - *Seminal nerve growth factor (NGF):* circulating NGF rose 3- to 6-fold within 15 min of semen deposition, mostly
    of uterine origin; cyclooxygenase inhibitors cut both NGF and ovulation (S-09). NGF and its receptors occur
    throughout the ovary, oviduct, and uterus (S-10).
  - *Contradiction:* injected rabbit or llama seminal plasma did not induce ovulation in individually caged does
    (S-11). In the same study's first experiment, group-housed saline-control does ovulated at an unexpectedly
    high rate, without males; with individually caged does, only GnRH-treated does ovulated (S-11). Pseudopregnancy also follows mounting by another doe (WikiVet, #93, `[LEAD]`).
  - *Revised-model watch:* the "coital reflex" model is being revised toward combined neural, endocrine, and social
    triggers; no consensus. *Medium.*
- **Pseudopregnancy** after sterile mating lasts 15–17 days (S-01) or about 18 days (S-14); the corpus luteum
  regresses from about day 12 under a uterine luteolytic factor, likely prostaglandin (S-01). Domestic does living
  with vasectomized males showed bouts of sexual and nest-related behavior about every 6 days during
  pseudopregnancy that the authors judged "not a manifestation of an underlying inherent sequential cycle or a
  typical mammalian oestrous cycle" (S-14). *Medium.*

**Breeding season and its controls**
- **Native range (*algirus*, southern Portugal):** breeding from November to June, peaking in March–April; every
  female examined from March to June was pregnant and/or lactating; testicular function correlated with
  temperature and with the water content of vegetation, and gonadal development in both sexes with vegetation
  biomass (S-06). *Medium.*
- **German enclosure:** first conceptions in early March, first litters early April (range 9 March–25 April), last
  litters at the end of September; season 207 ± 4 days (range 173–237) over 14 years (S-02). The season started
  with the appearance of green vegetation (S-02).
- **South-western Australia (Mediterranean climate):** breeding starts when winter rain germinates pasture
  (April–May) and stops when pasture dries; unseasonal summer rain can trigger limited breeding; some fertile males
  exist all year (fewest at midsummer); coastal sites out-produce inland ones (S-05). The authors conclude that
  "the presence of growing vegetation" decides *whether* rabbits breed, and a seasonal fertility cycle sets
  *intensity* (S-05). Farm data: wild does breed late winter to early summer; 16 h of light largely abolishes
  seasonality in domestic does; does mate more readily on 16 h light and bucks produce more sperm on 8 h (S-01).
- *Mechanism summary:* a photoperiod-linked fertility cycle (stronger in predictable northern climates) overlain
  by a nutritional trigger (green, high-protein, high-water forage). Which dominates varies with climate (S-05,
  S-06, S-02; Boyd & Bray 1989 via #3, [SN]). *High that both act; Medium on the balance.*

**Litter size, gestation, birth**
- **Gestation about 30–31 days** (30 d in S-02; ~31 d in veterinary sources, #94). Implantation at about day 7;
  eggs reach the uterus ~72 h after ovulation (S-01). *High.*
- **Litter size varies by population** (record all):
  - Bayreuth enclosure: 4.83 ± 0.04 (range 1–9, n = 1,575 litters), largest in the 2nd–3rd reproductive cycle of
    the season; litter size not affected by female age or rank (S-02).
  - Pancas, Portugal (*algirus*): 3.90 ± 0.50 by embryo count (S-06).
  - Australasian network: litter size and pregnancy rate vary with maternal age and time of year; adult litter size
    is reached at about 43 weeks of age (S-04).
  - Farm: 1–20, mostly 3–12, production average 7–9 (S-01).
  - Early-season litters are smaller; young from small litters grow up more aggressive (S-41).
- **Litter mass trade-off.** Litter mass at birth rises linearly with litter size (51 g for one pup; 335 g for
  nine), but individual birth mass falls as litter size rises (S-02). Implantation site matters: pups from the
  ends of the uterine horn are heavier at birth and survive better (S-47, domestic). Litter mass of 335 g for nine
  pups gives about 37 g per pup (*Derived*).
- **Kindling** lasts about 15–30 min (S-01); parturition "usually lasts only a few minutes" in another review
  (S-31). *Medium; conflict noted.*

**Postpartum estrus and concurrent pregnancy**
- **Does can conceive right after giving birth** ("postpartum oestrus"; S-02 citing Brambell 1948; S-21; S-104
  describes sexual behavior during postpartum estrus in wild rabbits). Concurrently pregnant and lactating does
  wean the first litter shortly before the next birth, stopping nursing at about postpartum day 26 (S-31, S-21).
  Up to 7 litters a season are theoretically possible at 30-day intervals, but the Bayreuth mean was 3.18 ±
  0.05 litters per female per year (range 0–6, n = 497), and the share of females breeding fell from about 80%
  in the first three cycles to under 10% in the last (S-02). *High.*
- **Farm data on receptivity:** receptivity is high just after kindling and depressed during lactation, attributed
  to prolactin antagonizing gonadotropin release; short doe–litter separation restores it; concurrent pregnancy and
  lactation put does in negative energy balance (#16, [SN], domestic). Pregnant does often accept mating,
  especially in the second half of pregnancy (S-01). *Medium.*

**Superfetation (revised-model watch)**
- **Not established in *O. cuniculus*.** Rabbits appear on lists of species "claimed" to show superfetation, but
  the evidence I found is one experimentally induced case (re-ovulation and re-insemination four days after
  mating; Wislocki & Snyder, via a review, [SN]) and one postulated case in a single New Zealand Black doe whose
  primary diagnosis was obstructive dystocia (S-19, [SN]). A critical review finds that no mammal has been shown to
  have superfetation proper as an evolved strategy, and that severe reproductive pathologies are often mistaken for
  it (S-15). The documented natural case is the brown hare, *Lepus europaeus*: superconception in late pregnancy,
  raising output per season by up to 35.4% (S-16); one paper calls the hare "the only species with superconception"
  (S-17); 59% of pregnant captive hares kept with a male showed it (S-18). *Contrast only.* *High that it is not
  documented in rabbits.*

**Nest building and its hormonal control (lab)**
- Sequence: the doe digs a nest burrow during pregnancy, carries straw shortly before birth, and lines the nest with
  hair she pulls from her chest and flanks (S-22, S-31). Veterinary timing: digging 6–8 days before kindling,
  nest 1–3 days before, hair pulling in the last 24 h (#94, `[LEAD]`).
- *Mechanism:* rising progesterone during pregnancy suppresses estrous chin-marking and stimulates digging, acting
  through progesterone receptors; the fall of progesterone before birth triggers straw-carrying and hair-pulling
  (S-22). Estradiol acting in the nucleus accumbens and medial preoptic area / bed nucleus of the stria terminalis
  drives digging; the preoptic site also drives straw-carrying (S-23). Prolactin is needed in late pregnancy:
  bromocriptine cut nest building from 77% to 19% of does, and brain-infused prolactin restored it to 93% (S-24).
  Androgens loosen ventral hair and cut food intake by 60–70% (S-25). The estrogen/progesterone ratio makes hair
  easier to pluck (#94, `[LEAD]`). Removing the corpus luteum late in pregnancy triggers nest building (Zarrow,
  1960s, via #94, [SN]). *High (lab).*
- **Stress disrupts it.** Does with a cortisol rise around parturition built nests later, had smaller litters, and
  lost more kits, though nest quality was unchanged (S-27). In wild-stock rabbits, a progesterone-receptor promoter
  variant was associated with when hay-carrying began; its frequency ran opposite to domestic rabbits; heritability
  0.10 (S-28). *Medium.*

**Mating system**
- Wild rabbits live in small territorial breeding groups (Bayreuth: 2–3 males and 3–5 females per group, range
  1–5 and 1–6) with separate, linear rank orders in each sex, set by fighting (S-02, S-03, S-08). High-ranking
  females have higher fecundity and lower offspring mortality, and rank-1 and rank-2 females live about one season
  longer, giving about 60% higher lifetime reproductive success (S-02, S-03). Females that had litter sisters in
  their group started breeding about 14 days earlier (S-42). Australian groups hold "two to ten or more" adults
  (S-07). The literature calls the system polygynous (S-08) or "polygynandrous" (Animal Diversity Web, #134,
  `[LEAD]`). **No genetic study of multiple paternity within rabbit litters was found** (#97, #98, #134, #96).
  *Medium (structure); paternity unknown.*

**Lactation and nursing**
- **Brief, infrequent nursing is the only mother–young contact.** Nursing lasts about 3 min (2–4 min; S-20, S-29;
  under 5 min, S-34). The doe stands almost motionless over the litter (S-31), leaves "mostly with a jump" (S-32),
  and closes the burrow behind her (S-31).
- **"Once a day" is approximate (revised-model watch).** On outdoor free range with infrared video, wild-stock does
  nursed 1.28 ± 0.54 times per 24 h and domestic does 1.12 ± 0.49; one nursing per day on 68.3% (wild) and 86.8%
  (domestic) of days, two on 26.9% and 11.2%; frequency peaked in the second week of lactation (S-32). Earlier
  "strictly once daily" reports used mostly direct observation (S-32). Wild-stock bouts were shorter (178.5 vs
  211.8 s), but total daily nursing time was similar (~230–240 s) (S-32). Wild does nursed mostly 3–6 h after dusk
  (peak 01:00–02:00), domestic does mostly in the first hours after dusk (peak 19:00–21:00) (S-32; note the
  paper's French summary reverses the two, and the English results and figure are followed here).
- **Mechanism of timing: circadian vs hourglass (disputed).** Lab work shows daily nursing entrains PER1 rhythms in
  the doe's paraventricular, supraoptic, and preoptic nuclei and pre-nursing activation of mesolimbic dopamine cells
  (S-29); paraventricular lesions disrupt timing (S-29). A suckling threshold is needed: litters under six kits
  disrupt circadian nursing (S-29), and procedures that cut nipple stimulation disrupt timing but not maternal
  motivation (S-20). But mothers with free access nurse with a period consistently *shorter than 24 h* (about 23 h)
  regardless of light cycle, while their feeding stays entrained; when pups are removed, visits stop within about a
  day (S-30, S-31). The authors propose an hourglass process reset at each nursing (S-30, S-31). *Medium; open.*
- **Milk is concentrated.** Farm data: milk is about 26% dry matter, 13.5% protein, 9.5% fat (rising to 20–22%
  after week 3), and under 1% lactose; yield rises from 30–50 g/day in the first two days to 200–250 g/day at the
  end of week 3, then falls; oxytocin release is proportional to the number of suckling young; prolactin drives
  synthesis (S-01). Pups can drink up to a quarter of their body mass in one 3–4 min bout (S-31); another review
  gives about one sixth of body weight per day (#9, [SN]). *Medium–High; intake figure varies.*
- **Weaning and its termination.** Lactating-only does keep nursing past postpartum day 44 and do not wean on their
  own; concurrent pregnancy (by unknown factors, not progesterone) triggers abrupt weaning (S-21, S-20).
- **Under drought,** estimated milk output of lactating wild does fell below what a litter needs (S-99).

**Intrauterine loss**
- Whole-litter losses in the uterus are common in wild populations: one British series found about 60% of
  litters lost entirely (Brambell 1942; S-55, [SN] via a Springer chapter summary); S-02 attributes the gap between
  possible and realized litters to such losses (citing Brambell, McIlwaine 1962, Mykytowycz & Fullagar 1973, Boyd
  & Myhill 1987, Parer & Libke 1991), and cites lower intrauterine mortality in dominant females (Mykytowycz 1960).
  Farm: only 60–70% of ova become live young, with most loss in the last 15 days of gestation (S-01).
  *Medium (the 60% figure is secondary).*

**Stress and reproduction**
- In the Bayreuth population, physiological costs of social position came mainly from psychosocial stress, much
  lower in high-ranking animals; offspring of subordinate (stressed) mothers are often stunted (S-03, S-02).
  *Medium.*

### 11 · Development and maturation

- **Altricial young, born in a closed nest.** Pups are born furless with closed eyes in an underground nest
  (S-48, S-34); eyelids open around postnatal day 10–12 (lab; see Log 01 S-10). They leave the natal burrow at
  about day 20; weaning ends around day 30, when the doe usually gives birth again (S-02). Farm weaning is 26–28
  days (intensive) to 5–6 weeks (extensive) (S-01); Hoy & Selzer weaned at 28 days (S-32).
- **Kits anticipate nursing.** From birth, pups show an endogenous rhythm of anticipatory activity, uncovering from
  nest material 1–2 h before nursing (S-20, S-31). Activity is U-shaped around the nursing visit (S-35). Daily milk
  intake entrains pups' locomotion, corticosterone, metabolism, and the dorsomedial hypothalamus (whose clock
  protein PER1 follows feeding time while the suprachiasmatic nucleus stays night-phased) (S-34, S-29). *Medium–
  High (lab).*
- **Siblings are part of development.** Heavier pups take central huddle positions, stay warmer, get more milk,
  and convert it better (S-36, wild-encl + lab; S-45). Pups raised without littermates got less milk, gained less,
  developed balance later, and competed worse for food after weaning (S-49). In the field enclosure, pup growth was
  best explained by litter size (negative), maternal age (middle-aged mothers best), and whether the mother had just
  had another litter (S-37). Optimal litter size for growth depended on soil temperature: two pups when soil was
  above 10 °C, three when colder (S-38). Within-litter differences in skin temperature were repeatable, and warmer
  pups grew more; by days 12–17, pups showed correlated behavioral styles (S-40). Huddle position in early life
  predicts long-term metabolic rate (S-135). *High.*
- **Early life sets later fitness.** For 1,836 female pups, survival to maturity and lifetime reproductive success
  peaked in medium litters; lifetime success peaked with mothers aged 2–3 years; later-born females survived and
  reproduced less, likely because they had less time to grow before winter (S-39). Males from small litters (heavier
  as nestlings) fought more and won more in their first breeding season (S-43). *High.*
- **Diet and microbes come from the mother.** See domain 7 (S-50, S-51, S-52).
- **Puberty and first breeding vary with conditions** (record all):
  - Australasia: few rabbits pregnant before 19 weeks; full adult pregnancy rate at about 27 weeks (S-04). Wild
    rabbits "can reach sexual maturity with 3–5 months" in Australia and New Zealand (refs in S-02). Early-born
    subadults in south-western Australia breed in their birth year, creating a second pregnancy peak (S-05).
  - New Zealand (secondary): females mature at about 5 months, males about 9 months (#55, `[LEAD]`).
  - German enclosure: young do not breed until the next spring; mean age at "adult" status (1 March after birth)
    was 304 ± 2 days (n = 223) (S-02).
  - Farm: does reach puberty at 4–6 months (small/medium breeds) or 5–8 months (large), at 70–75% of adult
    weight; bucks show first sexual behavior at 60–70 days, first sperm in the ejaculate at about 110 days, and NZW
    sexual maturity (sperm output stops rising) at 32 weeks (S-01). Extra light and male presence advanced puberty
    from ~167 to ~142 days in a tropical study (#125, [SN]).
  - *Mechanism:* puberty is tied to reaching a fraction of adult mass (S-01) and gated by the breeding season
    (S-02, S-05). *High that it varies; Medium for each value.*
- **Juvenile dispersal.** Kittens under ~600 g stay on their birth warren; animals over 1,250 g have moved and joined
  adult groups (S-07). Philopatry among young ranged 18–90% (males) and 32–95% (females) across four Australian
  populations; male bias appeared in some populations but not all; distances fell as density rose; after a
  population crash, subadults of both sexes moved farther; at high density both sexes dispersed at about 20% (S-07).
  A 1992 study (authors unconfirmed) found juvenile males most mobile, with mean moves of 149 m (juvenile
  females) to 264 m (juvenile males) (#56, [SN]). In East Anglia, males disperse and females tend to stay (S-08).
  *High that dispersal is density-dependent and variably male-biased.* (Log 01 covers the social side.)

### 12 · Lifespan and aging

- **Most wild rabbits die young.** At Bayreuth, nestling mortality from birth to day 12 was about 40%, and only
  5.5% of neonates survived to adulthood over 14 years; litters born late in the season never produced adults
  (S-02). In Australia, survival to 24 weeks ranged 20–55% between populations (S-07 citing S-04); survival improves
  with age and becomes roughly constant after 24 weeks (S-04). *High.*
- **Adult female lifespan (enclosure):** mean reproductive lifespan 712 ± 37 days after reaching adulthood (range
  69–2,086); of 197 females, 130 reached a second season, 85 a third, 48 a fourth, 20 a fifth, and 8 a sixth (S-02).
  High-ranking females lived about one breeding season (50%) longer (S-02, S-03). *High.*
- **Maximum recorded wild lifespan:** 9.5 years, a female in South Australia (S-57), beating the same site's 7.6-year
  record (S-56). The authors note the longevity may be site-specific. *High (single individuals).*
- **Domestic (pets, coordinate only):** median age at death 4.3 years (IQR 2.1–7.0) among English pet rabbits that
  died during the study (S-58). Press coverage of the same study gives survival up to 14.4 years and longer life in
  males (5.2 vs 3.7 years) (#63, [SN]). *Medium.*
- **Reproductive aging.** Packer et al. list rabbits among mammals with documented reproductive cessation and suggest
  it may be a non-adaptive by-product of life history (S-53). In a farm line, performance fell after the third
  parity; a line selected for at least 25 parities showed later senescence linked to body reserves (S-54). Wild
  females rarely live long enough to show it (S-02). *Medium.*
- **Age-related disease (pets):** tumor prevalence at necropsy was 14.4% overall and 19.8% in intact females, rising
  to 47.2% and 66.7% respectively in rabbits over 6 years; uterine adenocarcinoma affected 13.1% of intact females
  (S-64). Uterine disorders were found in 26.8% of 854 female necropsies; adenocarcinoma metastasized in 46.2% of
  cases (to the lung in 79.1%), and uterine tumors increased with age (S-65). Adenocarcinoma was 54% of diagnosed
  uterine disease in 50 pet rabbits (S-133). *Medium–High (domestic only; no wild data).*

### 17 · Health and vulnerabilities

**Myxomatosis**
- **Origin and impact.** Myxoma virus is a mild infection of *Sylvilagus* cottontails in South America and
  California, but lethal in *Oryctolagus* (S-67). After release in the 1950s (Australia and Europe, S-68; France 1952 and Britain by 1953, S-08),
  it killed "almost every rabbit which became infected"; Britain's population fell by an estimated 99% in two years
  (S-67), with initial mortality up to 99.9% (S-08). No scientific record exists of the Iberian myxomatosis crash
  (S-72). *High.*
- **Host resistance evolved fast, then the virus answered.** In Australia, by 1958 (eight years after release),
  mortality from a moderately virulent (Grade IIIa) strain in rabbits from one area had fallen from an expected
  90% to 26%; by 1961–66 rabbits from one Victorian area resisted a fully virulent (Grade I) strain (mortality 100%
  → 68%), with no further gain by 1971–75; selected domestic rabbits plateaued after about six generations (all
  cited in S-67). Britain was slower, taking over 20 years: wild rabbits that had died at 84–94% from the Grade IIIa
  strain earlier died at 45% (Wiltshire, n = 71), 44% (Angus, n = 45), and 0% (Hampshire, n = 16) in 1978–79,
  against 100% in domestic controls; Wiltshire rabbits died at 45% from a Grade II strain (1979) and 52% from a
  Grade I strain (1980), where Norfolk rabbits had died at 100% in 1974–75 (S-67, [FT]). Museum exomes show parallel selection on standing variation at many immunity genes
  in Australia, France, and Britain, including three changes in IFN-α21A that boost its antiviral effect (S-68).
  Newer virus strains are strongly immunosuppressive (S-68, S-128, [SN]). *Mechanism: polygenic immune adaptation
  (host) and immunosuppressive virulence (virus).* *Revised-model watch:* the old "evolution toward mildness" story
  is revised to an ongoing arms race (S-128). *High.*
- **Still endemic in the native range.** In southern Spain, 59.9% of 2,376 wild rabbits (2009–2021) carried
  antibodies and 8.8% were PCR-positive; seropositivity was linked to autumn, age, recent outbreaks, temperature,
  humidity, and RHDV status (S-70). *High.*
- **The vector is tied to rabbit reproduction.** The rabbit flea *Spilopsyllus cuniculi* can mature its ovaries only
  on a pregnant doe; at birth fleas move to the nest, feed on the young, and lay eggs (S-75, [SN]). *Mechanism:*
  host reproductive hormones entrain the vector's breeding, which ties transmission to the breeding season.
  *Medium.*

**Rabbit hemorrhagic disease (RHD)**
- **Pathology.** A calicivirus (RHDV, GI.1) causes peracute or acute disease in 1–3 days: necrotizing hepatitis,
  disseminated intravascular coagulation, and hemorrhage. Australian wild rabbits died within 20–24 h of a standard
  dose versus 30–36 h for lab rabbits (S-66). *High.*
- **Age resistance (GI.1) and its mechanism.** Kittens under about 4 weeks show no clinical disease yet become
  immune; in lab-bred Australian wild rabbits, all kittens under 10 days survived inoculation, 6 of 13 at 5 weeks,
  and 2 of 18 at 7–9 weeks; virus load in adult livers was a million times higher than in kittens. The virus binds
  ABH histo-blood-group antigens on upper respiratory and gut mucosa, which are not fully expressed until about 6
  weeks, but protection persists when that route is bypassed, so another step must exist. Maternal IgG crosses the
  placenta and protects to about 13 weeks (S-66). *High.*
- **Revised for GI.2 (RHDV2).** GI.2, first recorded in France in 2010 (#27, [SN]), causes disease in kittens as young as 11
  days and kills young rabbits at much higher rates than GI.1 (S-69; Dalton et al. 2012 cited there; a Spanish
  challenge reported 84% mortality under 4 weeks, #27, [SN]). In Australia it was replacing the endemic strains within about 18
  months of arrival (S-69). *High.*
- **Population effects vary with climate.** RHD cut abundance by about 50% in Murcia (Spain) in 1990, 70% in
  south-western Australia, and over 90% at Gum Creek (Flinders Ranges), where numbers stayed below 15% of former
  levels; cool, wet regions were affected least (S-66). Doñana lost about 60% in the first wave (1990) and fell
  below 10% of the pre-RHD level by the time of the 2007 report (S-72). In central-southern Spain, 73.4% of 60 localities declined
  between 1993 and 2002 (S-71). The native-range species is now listed Endangered (#113, [SN]; assessment not
  read). Outbreak timing follows breeding, because new susceptible young and waning maternal antibodies drive
  epizootics (S-66). The virus survives weeks at 22 °C but only 3–7 days at 37 °C (S-66). *High.*

**Parasites**
- Predation and intestinal disease (several *Eimeria* coccidia and nematodes) were the main mortality factors at
  Bayreuth, and disease is "probably extremely underestimated" because predators remove sick animals (S-02).
  Hepatic coccidiosis (*E. stiedae*) was found in 24% of 87 shot wild rabbits in Cambridgeshire and was linked to
  juveniles in summer (S-73, [SN]). In a low-MHC-diversity population, one MHC class II allele lowered hepatic
  coccidia only in heterozygotes (S-74). Across 12 south-western Australian populations, parasite egg and oocyst
  counts were negatively related to survival (#115, [SN]). *Medium.*

**Gut and dental disease (domestic; coordinate only)**
- **Dental disease is common and diet-linked.** One-year prevalence 15.4% in 161,979 UK pet rabbits (cheek teeth
  13.7%, incisors 3.1%), rising with age and falling with body weight; not linked to lop ears or short skulls
  (S-60). It contributed to 51.2% of deaths among affected rabbits and most often presented as reduced food intake
  (25.1%) (S-61). Mechanism: loss of abrasive wear (S-63). *High.*
- **Gut stasis.** Ileus/gut stasis caused 4.3% of recorded deaths and anorexia 4.9% (S-58). Veterinary sources
  describe the chain: stress or pain → catecholamines → sympathetic inhibition of gut motility → anorexia → less VFA
  production → fat mobilization → hepatic lipidosis, often fatal within about a week; rabbits are said to be prone
  to acidosis (#65, `[LEAD]`; primary physiology not found). *Low–Medium.*
- **Flystrike (myiasis)** was the most common recorded cause of death in English pet rabbits (10.9%) (S-58). Perineal
  fecal soiling (4.5%) is a known precursor; it was more frequent in lop-eared and short-skulled rabbits (S-58, S-62).
- Enteritis risk is higher at 3 weeks than 6 weeks of age (E. coli; S-84), during the switch to cecotrophy.

**Stress responses**
- Social stress (S-03), cortisol around birth (S-27), and predator odor (fox odor raised corticosterone sharply in
  low-scanning individuals; S-85) all have measurable physiological effects. "Trancing" (tonic immobility) is a fear
  response with raised heart rate, respiration, and corticosterone in one study (S-83, [SN]); a 2014 study reported
  heart rate falling, and a 2021 study found less stress in miniature rabbits (#128, [SN]). Pain behavior was
  minimal one hour after tattooing even without anesthetic cream, though facial grimace scores rose (S-82, [SN]).
  Claims of death from trancing are anecdotal only. *Low–Medium.*

### Cross-domain · Population dynamics and fecundity

- **High potential, low realized recruitment.** Bayreuth produced 58–220 litters (274–1,116 nestlings) per year from
  ~36 adult females, yet only 5.5% of neonates reached adulthood (S-02). Lifetime: 7.68 ± 0.52 litters and 34.9 ±
  1.9 young per female, but only 47.2% of females left any adult offspring (S-02).
- **Annual production varies three-fold or more:** 53 young per adult female per year at Wairarapa (NZ) versus 17 in
  semi-arid and 15 in subalpine Australia (S-04); 15–38 across Australian populations (S-07 citing S-04). Bayreuth
  realized 3.18 litters × 4.83 young ≈ 15 young per female per year (*Derived*). Farm systems reach 30–60 weaned
  young per doe per year (S-01) — a coordinate showing the ceiling when food, predation, and season are removed.
- **Density dependence.** Distances moved and dispersal rates fall as density rises (S-07); overwinter survival is
  density-dependent (S-44); a density-dependent reproduction study exists (S-136, [TI]; not read).
- **Life-table position (Derived, for the allometry agent):** gestation ~30 days, litters of ~4–5 (wild),
  multiple litters per season through postpartum estrus, maturity in ~4–10 months depending on birth season, and
  typical adult life of 1–3 breeding seasons — a strongly "fast" life history for a 1–2 kg mammal. The brown hare
  contrast (S-16–S-18) is a related leporid with a ~41-day gestation (S-18) and precocial young born above ground
  (#130, `[LEAD]`). This is consistent with rabbit altriciality being linked to burrow nesting, but no
  phylogenetic test was found, and hare body mass was not sourced here. A contrast observation, not a rabbit trait.

### Mechanism highlights for M-1 (input, not Step C)

These are the findings most likely to force coherence decisions in a human-scale rabbit-human hybrid. Each is a
mechanism with its source; none is a ruling.

1. **Reproduction ↔ body size.** The rabbit's fast reproduction rests on several separable mechanisms: induced
   ovulation (S-09–S-13), no regular cycle (S-01, S-14), postpartum estrus allowing concurrent pregnancy and
   lactation (S-02, S-21), short gestation with altricial young in a burrow nest (S-02, S-48), very concentrated milk
   allowing ~3 min of nursing per day (S-01, S-32), and pregnancy-triggered abrupt weaning (S-21). Induced ovulation
   is not tied to small size: the reviews list ferrets, cats, and camelids as induced ovulators too (S-13). Litter
   size, gestation length, altriciality, and the once-daily nursing economy are tied to the small, burrow-nesting body
   and will not scale up without a decision (*Derived*: my inference from the sourced mechanisms). Flag for M-1
   and M-7.
2. **Diet ↔ gut.** Hindgut fermentation with a colonic separation mechanism, cecotrophy, short transit, a hard fiber
   floor (≥10% indigestible fiber), and continuously growing teeth whose eruption tracks wear (S-01, S-63, S-84,
   S-86). The published rationale for cecotrophy is a small-body constraint (S-88, S-89), and leporids are already
   the largest cecotrophs bar the coypu (S-87). A human-sized cecotroph would lie outside every known case; the
   mechanism exists, but its scaling is a handwave candidate for `HANDWAVES.md`. Teeth that never stop growing
   require an abrasive diet to stay healthy (S-63, S-60).
3. **Activity ↔ vision.** The clock is nocturnal-type (S-33) and the retina rod-rich (S-108, Log 01), but expressed
   activity runs from strictly nocturnal to two-peaked crepuscular depending on predators, weather, and season
   (S-101–S-104). "Crepuscular" is one condition-dependent outcome, not a fixed trait. Sleep is only known from lab
   animals (S-105–S-107).
4. **Thermoregulation ↔ size.** The ears are AVA-rich radiators under central neural control (S-109–S-112); heat
   tolerance is poor (S-01, S-114). Ear heat loss depends on surface-to-volume ratio, which falls as body size rises
   (*Derived* from the physics, not from a rabbit study). Flag for M-1.
5. **Disease ↔ reproduction.** Both major viral diseases are synchronized to the breeding season: myxomatosis through
   a flea whose breeding needs the doe's pregnancy hormones (S-75), and RHD through the yearly influx of susceptible
   young (S-66). Population-level resistance evolved within decades (S-67, S-68).

## Open research gaps

Each gap records where the search died (see Search log). None of these is an absence of the trait.

1. **Cecotrophy in the wild:** when and where wild rabbits produce and eat cecotrophs (burrow vs surface; time of
   day). Died at sources (#33).
2. **Wild sleep:** no EEG or behavioral sleep study in wild or semi-natural rabbits; bout structure in lab rabbits
   needs the full texts of S-105 and S-106.
3. **Forepaw use and manipulation:** no rabbit study of grasping, food handling, grooming kinematics, digging
   mechanics, or paw preference (#75, #78, #79). Only one osteological source on forearm rotation (S-119).
4. **Skeleton and spinal fragility:** no primary measurement of skeletal mass fraction or vertebral strength
   (#68).
5. **Body mass by population and sex:** need S-123's tables (Digital.CSIC copy failed) and the Lemnos study
   (bot wall) (#117–#119). Sexual size dimorphism direction is unresolved.
6. **Multiple paternity:** no genetic paternity study of wild rabbit litters (#96–#98, #134); the Bora, Kaiser &
   von Holst 2001 abstract may hold data.
7. **Warren microclimate:** no measured temperature buffering of warrens relative to the surface (#84).
8. **Heat and cold limits in wild rabbits:** thermoneutral zone and upper critical temperature come from farm NZW
   sources and secondary citations (S-114 not read; #83, #135).
9. **Primary tooth-growth rates:** only veterinary handout figures (#32).
10. **Toxic plants:** no peer-reviewed rabbit plant-toxicity review; pyrrolizidine tolerance unknown (#105, #106).
11. **GI stasis physiology:** the stress → ileus → hepatic lipidosis chain rests on veterinary CPD sources (#65).
12. **Annual adult survival rates:** Gilbert et al. 1987 life tables (S-04) need the full text (#60).
13. **Brambell 1942:** the intrauterine-loss proportions and timing need the primary paper (#17).
14. **Wild nursing timing:** Rödel et al. 2012 (wild diurnal nursing) and Lloyd & McCowan 1968 (Skokholm) were
    cited by S-31 but not found directly (#133).
15. **Breed size vs litter size:** no clean allometric mapping across domestic breeds (#131, #132).
16. **Eye opening and nest emergence in the wild:** only lab (Log 01) and breeder sources (#53, #54).
17. **Iberian myxomatosis crash:** S-72 states that no scientific record exists; this may be a permanent gap.
18. **Size scaling of cecotrophy:** the full text of S-88 (and S-87) would give the body-size threshold argument
    in numbers; currently abstract-level only (#35, #36).
